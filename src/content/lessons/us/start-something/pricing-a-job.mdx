## A two-hour booking is six hours of work

A rate of $60 an hour sounds serious. An <Term id="hourly-rate">hourly rate</Term> pays for the hours you name, and a match is not two hours of work.

Shooting is two hours. Sorting and editing the set is about three. Travel there and back is one. That is six hours of work for a two-hour booking.

At $120 for the session, those six hours pay $20 an hour, before one cost comes off. **The price has to cover all six hours, not the two the client sees.**

So count the hours first, then build a price on them. It has three parts: what the session spends, the month it belongs to, and the tax on what is left.

## What one session spends

Travel, memory cards and a share of the online gallery come to about $45. That is the <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: every extra session adds another $45.

Other costs arrive whether or not you shoot. A used camera and lens are being paid off at $150 a month. Editing software is $30, a share of the phone bill $40, and a portfolio site with insurance $50. Those <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term> come to $270 a month.

At $225 a session you keep $180 of it. That is your <Term id="margin">margin</Term>, and it pays down the $270 before any of it is yours. The session that finishes that job is your <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point.

## Set the tax aside before you spend it

Money from your own work is taxable even when no form arrives. The IRS gig economy pages count cash, property and goods the same way.

<Term id="self-employment-tax">Self-employment tax</Term> pays for Social Security and Medicare. It applies once your <Term id="net-earnings">net earnings</Term> from self-employment reach $400 in a year. The IRS puts the rate at 15.3% for 2026.

So treat it as a cost of the job. The $180 the session leaves, less a 15.3% set-aside of $27.54, is $152.46. Over six hours that is about $25 an hour.

**A set-aside is a planning rule, not the final bill.** The IRS works the real figure from a year of net earnings, after every business cost. Income tax can apply on top.

## One price for the job, with a written scope

A <Term id="flat-fee">flat fee</Term> is one price for a whole job, agreed before the work starts. It suits work with a clear end, such as a match or a set of headshots.

Speed then works in your favour. Edit the set in half the time and the fee is the same, so each hour earns more. On an hourly rate that bill would have shrunk instead.

Write down what the fee includes: the match, 30 edited photos, delivery within a week. That short <Term id="scope-of-work">scope of work</Term> is what lets you price the extras rather than absorb them.

Hourly still fits some work. When nobody can say how long a job will run, the bill can follow the clock.

## What a deposit tells you

People will tell you a price sounds fair. It costs them nothing to say, and it settles nothing either way.

A booking tells you more. Ask for a <Term id="order-deposit">deposit</Term>, say a third of the fee, to hold a date. A club that pays it has agreed to the price with money rather than words.

A deposit can also protect the day, but only if you write down what happens when a booking is cancelled late. Put that in the scope.

If nobody books, there is still something to learn. Ask what stopped them. Sometimes the answer is the price. Often it is the date, the scope, or how few people have seen your work.

## Keep the records from the first job

Three numbers build the price. What the session spends, the month's fixed costs shared across the sessions you expect, and all six hours at the rate you want an hour to pay. Add the set-aside on top of that, rather than taking it out of your own pay later.

Then keep every receipt and a record of every payment. The IRS gig work pages set out the records to keep and the costs that can be deducted. They also cover the <Term id="estimated-tax">estimated tax</Term> that people working for themselves may owe during the year.
