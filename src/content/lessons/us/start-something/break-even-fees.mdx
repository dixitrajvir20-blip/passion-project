## What one print leaves behind

You draw illustrations, print them at a makerspace, and sell them on an online craft marketplace for $20 each. Paper, ink, a clear sleeve and a stiff mailer cost $5. Buyers pay for shipping on top.

That looks like $15 kept. But the payout is smaller. In this example the marketplace takes a flat $2 on every order. The payment processor that handles the buyer's card takes $1. Each is a <Term id="platform-fee">platform fee</Term>.

So one print leaves you $12. That is your real <Term id="margin">margin</Term>. The fees are a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: every new order pays them again.

**Count every fee charged per order as part of what one sale costs you.**

## Small next to $20, big next to $15

Three dollars is 15% of the $20 price. Set it against the $15 you thought you kept, and it is a fifth. The second comparison is the one that counts, because the fees come out of your margin.

Your month costs $180. That is $150 for a makerspace membership with a large printer, and $30 for a monthly plan on the marketplace. These are your <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>. The plan is a fee too, but it is charged by the month.

**Fees per order go in the cost of one sale. Fees per month go in fixed costs.**

Without the fees, 12 prints would cover the $180. With them, it takes 15. That 15th print is your <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point.

## When the fee is a percentage

Many marketplaces take a share of the price instead of a flat amount, or as well as one. Say a platform takes 10%. On a $20 print that is $2. Raise the price to $25 and the fee becomes $2.50.

So the $5 rise reaches you as $4.50. **With a percentage fee, the platform shares in every price rise.**

Read the fee page for what the percentage is taken from. Some platforms take it from the item price alone. Others also take it from the shipping the buyer pays, so the same 10% costs you more. Payment processors often charge a small percentage plus a few cents per order.

## Flat fees and small orders

A flat fee works the other way. It stays the same whatever the order is worth. In this shop, $3 of fees is a twentieth of a $60 order, and all of a $3 one.

So cheap things sold one at a time lose the most to flat fees. That is why small sellers bundle them into packs, set a minimum order, or sell them as add-ons to a bigger order.

The same test works for any fee you meet. Ask whether it is charged per order, per item, per month, or as a share of the price. Then put it in the right place in your sum.

## What the tax rules count

Selling your own prints for profit usually counts as working for yourself. The IRS works out your earnings from self-employment by taking your business costs off what came in. Platform fees are among those costs.

Once those earnings reach $400 in a year, <Term id="self-employment-tax">self-employment tax</Term> usually applies, whatever your age. The federal line for a <Term id="form-1099-k">Form 1099-K</Term> is far higher, so many small shops never get one. **The income counts whether or not a form arrives.**

So note every sale and every fee as you go. That habit is <Term id="bookkeeping">bookkeeping</Term>, and a spreadsheet is enough. State income tax rules vary by state. For the federal rules, check with the IRS.

## Read the payout, not the sale

The sale page shows what the buyer paid. The payout report shows what reached you after each fee. After your first few orders, set one against the other. If the payout is lower than your price minus your costs, a fee is missing from your sum.

Fees also change. When a platform announces new ones, put them into the cost of one sale. Then work out break-even again, before the new fees start.
