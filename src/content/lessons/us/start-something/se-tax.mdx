## Nothing is withheld, and it is still owed

At a job, your employer takes Social Security and Medicare out of every paycheck. Tutoring and reselling work differently. You are <Term id="self-employed">self-employed</Term>, so the full amount reaches you and nothing is sent on for you.

**That does not mean nothing is owed.** Once your <Term id="net-earnings">net earnings</Term> from your own work reach $400 in a year, <Term id="self-employment-tax">self-employment tax</Term> applies. The IRS writes the rule around that $400, not around your age.

Net earnings are what came in, minus the <Term id="business-expense">business expenses</Term> of doing the work. Shipping labels count. So does a platform fee. A jacket you listed and then kept does not.

## Why the rate is 15.3%

At a job, Social Security takes 6.2% of your pay and Medicare takes 1.45%. Together they are <Term id="fica">FICA</Term>, and your employer pays the same again on top.

Working for yourself, you are the worker and the employer at once. **So you pay both halves: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare, 15.3% in all.** That is the IRS rate as of 2026.

One step softens it. The IRS applies the rate to 92.35% of net earnings, not to all of them. The calculation works that through on the $5,000 from the top of the page.

## Income tax is a separate question

Self-employment tax pays for Social Security and Medicare. Income tax is a different tax, and it can come on top.

Whether you owe it depends on your total income for the year: your own work, any job, anything else. **Self-employment tax is worked out on its own, so it can be due in a year with little or no income tax.**

When you file, half of the self-employment tax comes off your income before income tax is figured. State rules are separate again and vary by state, and your state's tax agency sets them out.

## Set the share aside as it arrives

The tax is settled once a year, on your <Term id="tax-return">tax return</Term>. The money for it arrives a few dollars at a time. **So give the tax a line of its own, and move it the day each payment lands.**

A separate account keeps tax money from looking like spending money. Where you keep it is your choice, and this lesson does not pick one.

## Paying during the year

The IRS generally expects people who will owe $1,000 or more when they file to pay during the year. Those payments are <Term id="estimated-tax">estimated tax</Term>, and they cover income tax and self-employment tax together.

The four due dates are April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15. Paying too little along the way can mean a penalty, and the IRS lists exceptions. If you also have a job, the IRS says you can have more taken from that paycheck with a new <Term id="w-4">W-4</Term> instead.

**For your own numbers, go to the IRS pages rather than to a friend.** The sources below are where to start.

## Keep the record as you go

<Term id="bookkeeping">Bookkeeping</Term> here is one line per payment: the date, who paid, how much. Add one line per cost, and keep the receipt. The IRS asks gig workers to collect records and receipts during the year.

Income counts whether or not a form arrives for it. A marketplace sends some sellers a <Term id="form-1099-k">Form 1099-K</Term>, and the IRS says this income is reported either way, including when it is paid in cash.

At the end of the year those lines become a <Term id="schedule-c">Schedule C</Term>: what came in, what it cost, what is left. **Every cost you can show lowers the net profit the tax is worked out from.**
