## Nobody takes it out for you

At a job, your employer takes Social Security and Medicare out of every paycheck. Mowing lawns for neighbors is different. You are <Term id="self-employed">self-employed</Term>, so nothing is taken out and the full amount reaches you.

**That does not mean nothing is owed.** Once your <Term id="net-earnings">net earnings</Term> from working for yourself reach $400 in a year, <Term id="self-employment-tax">self-employment tax</Term> applies. The IRS says this holds at any age, and for students a parent claims as a dependent.

Net earnings are what came in, minus the <Term id="business-expense">business expenses</Term> of doing the work. Gas for the mower counts. So does trimmer line, or a fee an app takes. A pizza after a long day does not.

## Why the rate is 15.3%

At a job, Social Security takes 6.2% of your pay and Medicare takes 1.45%. Together they are <Term id="fica">FICA</Term>, and your employer pays the same again on top.

Working for yourself, you are the worker and the employer at once. **So you pay both halves: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare, 15.3% in all.** That is the IRS rate as of 2026.

One step softens it. The IRS applies the rate to 92.35% of net earnings, not to all of them. On the $2,000 from the top of the page, the tax comes to about $283.

## Income tax is a separate question

Self-employment tax pays for Social Security and Medicare. Income tax is a different tax, and it can come on top.

Whether you owe income tax depends on your total income for the year: side work, any job, and anything else. State income tax rules vary by state. **Self-employment tax is worked out on its own, so it can be due in a year with little or no income tax.**

So the money you set aside has two jobs. It covers self-employment tax first, and any income tax after that.

## Set it aside the day it lands

The tax is settled once a year, on your <Term id="tax-return">tax return</Term>. But the money for it arrives a few dollars at a time. **So give the tax a line of its own, and move it the day each payment lands.**

The table shows one July of mowing, with 15% of every payment set aside. That share is an example, not a rule. On the $680 left after costs, self-employment tax is about $96. So $120 covers it, with some left toward any income tax.

Keep the set-aside in a separate account, so it never looks like spending money. Where is your choice, and this lesson does not pick one.

## Paying during the year

The IRS generally expects people who will owe $1,000 or more when they file to pay during the year. These payments are <Term id="estimated-tax">estimated tax</Term>, and they cover income tax and self-employment tax together.

The due dates are April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15. Paying too little along the way can mean a penalty, and the IRS lists some exceptions. If you also have a job, the IRS says you can instead have more tax taken from your paycheck, with a new <Term id="w-4">W-4</Term>.

**For your own numbers, go to the IRS pages, not a friend or this lesson.** The gig economy tax center in the sources is the place to start.

## Keep the record as you go

<Term id="bookkeeping">Bookkeeping</Term> here can be one line per payment: the date, who paid and how much. Add one line per cost, and keep the receipt. The IRS asks gig workers to collect records and receipts during the year.

Every cost you can show lowers your net earnings, and with them the tax. **A record kept each week takes minutes. One rebuilt in March takes a weekend, and costs go missing.**
