## Sales are the top line

An <Term id="income-statement">income statement</Term> covers one stretch of time: a month, a quarter or a year. It asks three things in order: what came in, what it cost, and what was left. You may also see it called a profit and loss statement, or P&L.

The first line is <Term id="revenue">revenue</Term>. Many statements label it sales. In September the shop sold about 6,000 drinks at an average of $5, so revenue was $30,000.

**That is where the counting starts, not what the shop kept.** The Securities and Exchange Commission calls it the top line, and every line under it takes something away.

## Gross profit: what a cup leaves

Every drink uses beans, milk, a cup and a lid: about $1.75. Across September those came to $10,500, and added up they are the <Term id="cost-of-goods-sold">cost of goods sold</Term>, sometimes shortened to COGS.

Take it from revenue and you reach <Term id="gross-profit">gross profit</Term>, $19,500 for the month. That line tests the product on its own. **Once a cup has paid for its own beans and milk, is anything left?**

Here, yes. At $5 a cup with $1.75 of ingredients, each one leaves $3.25 toward everything else.

## Net profit: after every bill

Below gross profit come the costs of running the shop at all: rent, wages, utilities, card fees and insurance. Together they are <Term id="operating-expenses">operating expenses</Term>, $17,500 in September.

Take those off gross profit and you reach <Term id="net-profit">net profit</Term>, the bottom line. **A record $30,000 of sales left $2,000.**

Written as shares of sales, the shape is easier to carry in your head. Beans, milk and cups took 35 cents of every dollar, wages 30 and rent 20.

## Rent and wages do not fall with a quiet week

Split the costs a second way. Beans, milk and cups are a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: more when the shop sells more, less when it sells less. Card fees behave the same way, because they are a share of what customers pay.

Rent and insurance are a <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed cost</Term>. They are the same in a quiet January as in a busy September. Most of the wage bill is close to fixed too, because shifts are scheduled before anyone knows how the week will go.

**That is why a quiet month can end in a loss.** Rent, insurance and booked shifts are owed whatever the till takes.

## A record month can leave less

March sold $27,000 and left $2,550. September sold $3,000 more and left $550 less. Nothing went wrong at the till.

Read each line as a share of sales and the movement shows up. Cost of goods went from 32 cents in the dollar to 35, because bean and milk prices rose. Rent, wages and card fees each cost more in dollars, but they held the same share of sales.

**Gross profit rose by $1,140, and operating expenses rose by $1,690.** The gap between those two is the $550.

At March's 32 cents in the dollar, September's beans and milk would have cost $9,600 instead of $10,500. The month would have kept about $2,900.

## When the money arrives

The shop's statement counts a sale on the day the coffee is served, not the day it is paid. That is the <Term id="accrual-method">accrual method</Term>. Under the cash method, the sale would count when the money arrives instead.

So an office order served on 30 September and paid in October sits in September's revenue and not yet in the bank. The gap between profit on paper and money to hand is <Term id="cash-flow">cash flow</Term>.

**A month's profit and the money in the account are two different numbers.** Check both before spending what the statement shows.

## Read it every month

One statement is a snapshot. Twelve of them show which line is moving, and that is the thing to act on: a supplier's price, a lease, the schedule.

That takes <Term id="bookkeeping">bookkeeping</Term>: every sale and every cost written down as it happens, with the receipts behind them. The IRS says good records help you "monitor the progress of your business" and prepare financial statements.

Someone who runs a US business on their own, as a sole proprietor, files <Term id="schedule-c">Schedule C</Term>. It reads the same way, from gross receipts, the tax form's name for sales, down to net profit. Take tax questions to the IRS.
