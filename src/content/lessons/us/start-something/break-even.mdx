## What one shirt leaves

You print shirts for school clubs and local teams. A shirt sells for $18. The plain shirt, the ink, the bag it ships in and the <Term id="platform-fee">platform fee</Term> on each shirt come to $7.50.

So one shirt leaves $10.50. That is its <Term id="contribution-margin">contribution margin</Term>: the only part of the $18 that can pay for anything else.

Every item in that $7.50 is a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: it is charged again on the next shirt. The platform fee belongs there too, alongside the ink.

**Contribution is the price minus everything that one shirt used up.** If it came out at zero, no number of shirts would help. That is what <Term id="unit-economics">unit economics</Term> means.

## The month costs $900 whatever you sell

Three bills arrive whether you print one shirt or three hundred. The press that fixes each design onto a shirt, and its printer, are on a twelve-month payment plan at $600 a month. Design software is $60, and a storage unit for plain shirts and finished orders is $240.

Those are <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>: costs that do not change with how much you make.

**A payment plan on equipment is a fixed cost until the last payment.** The press is yours at the end. Until then the $600 is due in a month with no orders in it.

| The month, whatever you sell | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Press and printer, monthly payment | $600 |
| Design software | $60 |
| Storage unit | $240 |
| Fixed costs | $900 |

## How many shirts cover the month

Divide the month's fixed costs by what one shirt leaves, then round up. The Small Business Administration gives the same sum in its guide to start-up costs.

Here it lands at 86 shirts. That count is your <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point. The 86th shirt clears the month; the 85th leaves the month a little short.

**Round up, because part of a shirt cannot be sold.**

This is one month's answer, not a target for the year. It moves whenever a price, a cost or a monthly bill changes, which is often.

## When the payment plan ends

The twelfth payment is the last one. From the month after, fixed costs are $300 rather than $900, and break-even falls to 29 shirts.

Nothing about the shirt changed. The same $10.50 now has a third as much to cover.

**When a plan ends, the month's count drops with it.** A plan that spreads $7,200 over twelve months adds $600 to each of those months, which here is fifty-seven more shirts.

Leases, financing and monthly subscriptions all behave this way. Each one looks small by itself, and each stays in the month's costs until it ends.

## Past the 86th shirt

Up to break-even every shirt's $10.50 is spoken for. It goes to the press payment, the software and the storage.

Past that point nothing is left to cover. The 87th shirt adds its whole $10.50 to profit, and so does every shirt after it.

**Past break-even each shirt adds its whole contribution.** A month at 60 shirts is $270 down. A month at 120 is $360 up. The gap between them is sixty shirts, at $10.50 each.

## Checking the number each month

Do the sum before you send a quote, not after. A shirt discounted to $15 leaves $7.50 instead of $10.50, and the month then takes 120 shirts.

Redo the count whenever something moves: a supplier's price, a fee page, the last payment on a plan. Note each order and each cost as it happens, because that record is where the numbers come from.

That habit is <Term id="bookkeeping">bookkeeping</Term>, and a spreadsheet is enough for it. The IRS sets no required format. It asks only that your records make plain what the business took in and what it paid out. Keep the receipts and invoices that sit behind those entries.
