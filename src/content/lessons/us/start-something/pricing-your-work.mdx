## Two ways to put a price on a lawn

Your friend says to charge by the hour. It sounds fair and simple. But an <Term id="hourly-rate">hourly rate</Term> pays you for time, not for a finished lawn.

That has an odd effect. **The better you get, the less each lawn pays.** A lawn that took two hours in June takes an hour and a half by July, and the bill shrinks with it.

The other way is a <Term id="flat-fee">flat fee</Term>: one price for the job, agreed before you start. The speed is yours: finish sooner, get paid the same, and every hour earns more.

Hourly still has a place. When nobody knows how long a job will take, like tutoring before a final, the bill can follow the clock.

## What one lawn costs you

Before you pick a number, look at one lawn. Gas for the mower, trimmer line and yard bags come to about $6 each time. That is the <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: every extra lawn adds another $6.

Other costs arrive whatever the weather. You bought a used mower and trimmer, and you pay your aunt back $120 a month. Add $40 toward your phone and $20 for flyers. These <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term> come to $180 a month.

At $40 a lawn, you keep $34 from each one. That is your <Term id="margin">margin</Term>. **It pays off the $180 before any of it is yours.** The lawn that finishes that job is your <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point.

## The hours nobody pays for

Mowing, edging and bagging take two hours. The job also has hours nobody sees: biking there and back, texting to set a day, waiting out a wet morning. Call it half an hour a lawn.

Now do the sum your friend skipped. Mow 12 lawns a month, three a week, and each one carries $15 of the $180. **At $40, what is left for you is $19, for about two and a half hours.** That is under $8 an hour, not the $20 it looked like.

| --- | --- |
| Gas, trimmer line and bags | $6 |
| Share of monthly costs, at 12 lawns a month | $15 |
| Left for you, for about two and a half hours | $19 |

A price per job has to cover all of it: what the lawn uses up, its share of the month, and every hour it takes, seen or not.

## Price the job, then get faster

So start from the floor. Your friend's $15 an hour, paid for all two and a half hours, is $37.50. Add the $6 of supplies and the $15 share, and the lawn needs a price near $58.50. Then ask neighbors what they pay for the same lawn.

Write down what the price includes: front and back, edges trimmed, clippings bagged. That short <Term id="scope-of-work">scope of work</Term> lets you price the extras, like raking leaves in October.

Once the price is per job, two things raise your pay with no new customers. **Get faster, and each hour earns more.** Take lawns on the same street, and the ride between them shrinks.

## Tax is one of the costs

Money from mowing, tutoring or design is taxable income, even when nobody sends you a tax form. The IRS says so on its gig economy pages.

Two taxes can apply. Income tax depends on your total income. <Term id="self-employment-tax">Self-employment tax</Term> pays for Social Security and Medicare. It applies once your net earnings from self-employment reach $400 in a year. As of 2026, the IRS puts its rate at 15.3%. State income tax rules vary by state.

**So tax belongs in the price, the same way gas does.** Keep a record of every payment and every receipt, because some costs of the work can lower the tax owed. The IRS pages in the sources explain who files, and when.
