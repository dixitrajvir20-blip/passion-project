## An empty file is not a bad score

If you have never borrowed, a credit check comes back with almost nothing. That is being <Term id="credit-invisible">credit invisible</Term>: no history with any of the three nationwide bureaus, and nothing to score.

A file starts when a lender reports an account to a <Term id="credit-bureau">credit bureau</Term>. A debit card moves money you already have, so there is no borrowing to report. The CFPB says most utility and phone companies send no payment history either. For rent, its advice is to ask your landlord whether they join a rent-reporting program.

**What a file needs is one account that gets reported, paid on time, month after month.** After that, what matters is time. Scores read how accounts are handled over months and years.

## What the file records

A <Term id="credit-report">credit report</Term> lists each reported account: what you owe, your <Term id="credit-limit">credit limit</Term>, and whether each payment arrived on time.

A <Term id="credit-score">credit score</Term> is a formula run on that report. The CFPB calls it a prediction of how you handle credit, starting with whether a loan gets repaid on time. Most scores run from 300 to 850.

**You have several scores, not one.** Different formulas, and different bureaus, give different numbers on the same day.

Negative marks, such as a payment reported late, can generally stay for seven years. On-time payments can stay while you keep paying, which is why time does so much of the work here.

## What moves the number

| What the score reads | Helps | Hurts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payment history | Every payment by its due date | A payment reported late |
| Share of limits in use | Small balances, cleared monthly | Balances close to the limit |
| Age of accounts | Accounts kept open for years | Several accounts opened at once |
| New applications | Applying only when you need to | Applications close together |

The share of your limits you are using is your <Term id="credit-utilization">credit utilization</Term>. On a $600 limit, a $120 balance is 20% and a $480 balance is 80%. The second reads as stretched, even when every payment is on time.

Applying for credit adds a <Term id="hard-inquiry">hard inquiry</Term>. **Several applications close together can read to a lender as financial strain, the CFPB warns.** Checking your own report is a <Term id="soft-inquiry">soft inquiry</Term> and changes nothing.

## Interest starts when a balance carries

A card statement shows a <Term id="statement-balance">statement balance</Term> and a due date. On most cards you can pay that balance in full by the due date and owe no interest on purchases.

Leave any part of it and two things happen. Interest runs on the unpaid part, and anything you buy after that starts collecting interest the moment you buy it.

The <Term id="apr">APR</Term> is the yearly rate that interest is charged at. On new general purpose card accounts opened in 2024, the CFPB reported an average APR of 27.5%.

**A carried balance is not how a file gets built.** The account is reported either way.

## Reading your own file, free

Each of the three nationwide bureaus lets you pull your report once a week at no charge. The FTC says that has been made permanent. Those reports come from one place: annualcreditreport.com.

Look-alike sites register near-misses of that address, so it is worth typing the address rather than tapping an ad. Anything asking for payment is selling a service of its own.

**If something on a report is wrong, tell the bureau and the company that reported it.** The FTC says both have to correct an error, both have to do it free, and the bureau has 30 days to investigate.
