## An empty file is not a bad score

If you have never borrowed, a credit check comes back with almost nothing. That is being <Term id="credit-invisible">credit invisible</Term>: no history with any of the three nationwide bureaus, and nothing for a score to be worked out from.

A file starts when a lender reports an account to a <Term id="credit-bureau">credit bureau</Term>. A debit card is not usually reported. The CFPB says most utility and phone companies send no payment history either, and rent reaches a report only through a rent-reporting program.

**What a file needs is one account that gets reported, paid on time, month after month.** After that, the main ingredient is time. Scores read how accounts are handled over months and years.

## What the file records

A <Term id="credit-report">credit report</Term> lists each reported account: what you owe, your <Term id="credit-limit">credit limit</Term>, and whether each payment arrived on time.

A <Term id="credit-score">credit score</Term> is a formula run on that report. The CFPB calls it a prediction of how you handle credit, starting with whether a loan gets repaid on time. Most scores run from 300 to 850.

**You have several scores, not one.** Different formulas, and different bureaus, give different numbers on the same day.

Negative marks, such as a payment reported late, can generally stay for seven years. On-time payments can stay while you keep paying, which is why time does so much of the work here.

## What moves the number

| What the score reads | Helps | Hurts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payment history | Every payment by its due date | A payment reported late |
| Share of limits in use | Small balances, cleared monthly | Balances close to the limit |
| Age of accounts | Accounts kept open for years | Several accounts opened at once |
| New applications | Applying only when you need to | Applications close together |

The share of your limits you are using is your <Term id="credit-utilization">credit utilization</Term>. On a $600 limit, a $120 balance is 20% and a $480 balance is 80%. The second reads as stretched, even when every payment is on time.

Applying for credit adds a <Term id="hard-inquiry">hard inquiry</Term>. **The CFPB says that applying for a lot of credit in a short time can look to a lender like money trouble.** Checking your own report is a <Term id="soft-inquiry">soft inquiry</Term> and changes nothing.

## Interest starts when a balance carries

A card statement shows a <Term id="statement-balance">statement balance</Term> and a due date. Most cards give you until that date to pay in full, and on purchases there is then no interest to pay at all.

Leave any part of it and two things happen. Interest runs on the unpaid part, and anything you buy in the new cycle starts collecting interest the moment you buy it.

The <Term id="apr">APR</Term> is the yearly rate that interest is charged at. On new general purpose card accounts opened in 2024, the CFPB reported an average APR of 27.5%. The calculation below is what that costs on $1,000.

## Reading your own file, free

The three nationwide bureaus have made weekly free reports permanent, the FTC says. Those reports come from one place: annualcreditreport.com. Anything else offering them is selling something of its own.

Imposter sites copy that name, sometimes with a deliberate misspelling, and exist to sell you services or to collect your personal details. Type the address yourself rather than tapping an ad.

**If something on a report is wrong, tell the bureau and the company that reported it.** The FTC says both have to correct an error, both have to do it free, and the bureau has 30 days to investigate.

## Paying before you have seen the place

A first apartment is also when rental scams arrive. From January 2020 to June 2025 the FTC logged nearly 65,000 rental scam reports and about $65 million lost. Half of those who lost money lost $1,000 or more. Renters aged 18 to 29 reported losses at three times the rate of older adults.

The pattern is money before access: an application fee, a deposit or first month's rent for a place you have not been inside. The reason given is distance, or another applicant about to take it.

**The FTC's advice is to see the place, in person or on a video tour, and to match the owner's name against city or county records before any money moves.**
