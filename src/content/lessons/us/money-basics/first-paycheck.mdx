## From $500 to $451.75

Forty hours at $12.50 is $500. That is your <Term id="gross-pay">gross pay</Term>: what the job pays before anything comes off. What reaches your account is <Term id="net-pay">net pay</Term>, here $451.75.

The $48.25 in between is not a mistake, and it is not lost. It is tax, taken out by your employer and sent on for you. Every line is on your <Term id="pay-stub">pay stub</Term>.

**Plan the month with net pay, because it is the only part you can spend.** Your hourly rate still matters. It is the number in job ads, and the one a raise is measured against.

## Two lines on almost every paycheck

In 2026, Social Security takes 6.2% of your pay and Medicare takes 1.45%. Some stubs group the pair as <Term id="fica">FICA</Term>. Your employer pays the same amounts again, on top of your pay.

Social Security pays an income when people retire, or when a disability stops them working. Medicare is health insurance, mostly for people 65 and older.

**These two come out even when you owe no income tax at all.** On this paycheck they take $31 and $7.25. They are not part of any income tax refund later.

## Federal income tax is an estimate

When you start a job, you fill in a <Term id="w-4">W-4</Term>. Your employer uses it, with the IRS tables, to decide how much federal income tax to take from each paycheck. That is <Term id="withholding">withholding</Term>.

The tables treat each paycheck as if you were paid it all year. For a single filer with only the basics on the W-4, the 2026 tables take nothing until pay passes about $620 every two weeks. That is why this stub shows $0.

**So withholding is a guess, not your final bill.** Work one summer at $1,000 a paycheck, and more can come out than you end up owing.

## Getting it back, and the exempt box

After the year ends, your employer sends you a W-2, showing your pay and the tax withheld. You can then file a <Term id="tax-return">tax return</Term>, which works out what you really owed. **If too much was withheld, filing is how the extra comes back.** That is true even when you are not required to file.

The W-4 also has an exempt option. It fits only if you owed no federal income tax last year and expect to owe none this year. It lasts one calendar year, so a new W-4 goes in by February 15 to keep it.

Exempt stops federal income tax withholding. Social Security and Medicare still come out.

## State tax, and lines you do not know

State income tax follows your state's rules, not federal ones. How much comes out depends on your pay and the rate where you live. Some cities and counties add their own. So a friend's stub from another state tells you little about yours. Your state's tax agency explains its lines.

Some stubs carry other lines too, such as health insurance or a retirement plan through work. **If a line makes no sense, ask payroll what it is.** It is an ordinary question, and they hear it often.

## Read it in the first week

Three checks, while the two weeks are still fresh. First, the hours and the rate, against your own notes and your offer. Second, each line from gross down to net. Third, the YTD (year to date) column, which adds up every paycheck since the year began.

Then give the net pay its jobs before the first spend. <Term id="needs-and-wants">Needs and wants</Term> come first to mind, but savings works best when it moves on payday. Two paychecks like this one leave about $900 for the month, not $1,000.
