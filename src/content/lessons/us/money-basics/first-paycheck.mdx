## From $1,600 to $1,317.60

Eighty hours at $20 is $1,600. That is your <Term id="gross-pay">gross pay</Term>: what the job pays before anything comes off. What reaches your account is <Term id="net-pay">net pay</Term>, here $1,317.60.

The $282.40 in between is not a fee and it is not lost. Each line is a <Term id="deduction">deduction</Term>: an amount your employer takes out and sends on for you. All four are named on your <Term id="pay-stub">pay stub</Term>.

**Plan the month on net pay, because it is the only part you can spend.** The hourly rate still matters. It is the figure in job ads, and what a raise is measured against.

Those last two amounts are examples from one stub: yours depend on your W-4 and your state.

## Social Security and Medicare are fixed rates

In 2026 Social Security takes 6.2% of your pay and Medicare takes 1.45%. Stubs often group the pair as <Term id="fica">FICA</Term>. Your employer pays the same amounts again, on top of your pay. That 6.2% applies to the first $184,500 you earn in a year, well above a first job.

Social Security pays retirement income, and disability income before that. Medicare is health insurance from age 65, or earlier with a disability or certain serious illnesses.

**These two come out even when you owe no income tax at all.** On this stub they take $99.20 and $23.20. They are separate taxes from income tax, not an advance on it.

## Federal income tax is an estimate

When you start a job you fill in a <Term id="w-4">W-4</Term>. Your employer uses it, with tables from the IRS, the federal tax agency, to set the federal income tax taken from each paycheck. That is <Term id="withholding">withholding</Term>.

The tables work a year at a time. They multiply one paycheck by the number of paydays in a year, then tax that figure. A summer of $1,600 paychecks is read as a whole year of them, so more can come out than you end up owing.

**Withholding is an estimate, not your final bill.** Your employer sends a <Term id="w-2">W-2</Term> after the year ends. A <Term id="tax-return">tax return</Term> works out what you really owed and brings back anything extra.

## The exempt box on the W-4

The W-4 also has an exempt option. It fits only if you owed no federal income tax last year and expect to owe none this year. It lasts one calendar year, so a new W-4 is due by February 15 to keep it.

**Exempt stops federal income tax withholding, and nothing else.** Social Security and Medicare still come out, and so does any state tax.

## State income tax depends on where you live

State income tax follows your state's rules, not federal ones. What comes out depends on your pay and on the rate where you live. Some places add a local tax on top.

Not every state has one. Washington is among those without: its revenue department reports no individual income tax there at the moment. A friend's stub from another state therefore tells you little about yours, and your state's tax agency sets out the rules.

A stub can carry other lines too, for things you signed up for at work. **If a line makes no sense, ask payroll what it is.**

## Read the stub in the first week

Three checks, while the two weeks are still fresh. First, the hours and the rate, against your own notes and your offer. Second, each line from gross down to net. Third, the YTD column, short for year to date, which adds up every paycheck since January.

Then plan the net pay before the first spend. Sort it into <Term id="needs-and-wants">needs and wants</Term>, and set the savings share on payday rather than from what is left over. Two paychecks like this one make a month of about $2,635, not $3,200.
