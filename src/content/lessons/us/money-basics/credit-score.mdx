## A score reads a report

Once you have a card or a loan, the lender sends updates to a <Term id="credit-bureau">credit bureau</Term>. Each month adds to your <Term id="credit-report">credit report</Term>: what you owe, your limit, and whether the payment came on time.

A <Term id="credit-score">credit score</Term> is a formula run on that report. It guesses how likely you are to pay the next bill on time. Most scores run from 300 to 850.

**You have many scores, not one.** Different formulas and different bureaus give different numbers on the same day. They tend to move together, because they read the same kinds of facts.

Those facts come down to four things. Each one works like a mechanism, not a grade.

| What the score reads | Helps | Hurts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paying on time | Every payment by its due date | A missed or late payment |
| Share of your limit in use | Low balances, paid in full | Balances near the limit |
| Age of your accounts | Accounts kept open for years | Many brand-new accounts |
| New applications | Applying only when you need to | Several applications close together |

## On time comes first

Paying on time is the biggest thing a score reads. Every statement shows a due date and a <Term id="minimum-payment">minimum payment</Term>. **Paying at least the minimum by the due date counts as on time.** The rest can follow, though it usually collects interest.

Miss the date and the card can add a <Term id="late-fee">late fee</Term>. A payment that goes unpaid can also be reported to the bureaus as late. Most marks like that can stay on a report for up to seven years.

So the date matters more than the amount. The full balance paid three days late is still late. The minimum paid a day early is on time.

## How much of the limit you use

Your <Term id="credit-limit">credit limit</Term> is the most the card lets you owe. The share of it you are using is your <Term id="credit-utilization">credit utilization</Term>. On a $500 limit, a $450 balance is 90% and a $150 balance is 30%.

**A high share reads as stretched, even when every payment is on time.** A common rule of thumb, which the CFPB repeats, is to stay at or under 30% of your total limit.

Paying the full balance each month keeps the share low. You do not need to carry a balance to build a score. A balance left unpaid adds interest and nothing else.

## Old accounts and new applications

A longer record of on-time payments helps, so the age of your accounts counts. An old card with no yearly fee does quiet work just by staying open. Closing it also takes its limit out of your total, so the same balance becomes a bigger share.

Each application for credit adds a <Term id="hard-inquiry">hard inquiry</Term> to your report. Scores look at how recently and how often you apply. **Several applications close together can read as a need for credit.**

Shopping for one loan is treated differently. For a car loan, checks made within 14 to 45 days of each other generally count as one inquiry. So comparing lenders inside a short window costs little or nothing.

## What does nothing, and where to look

**Checking your own report or score never lowers it.** That is not an application, so it is not a hard inquiry.

All three nationwide bureaus give you your report free, every week, at AnnualCreditReport.com. The FTC says it is the one official site for the free reports that federal law gives you. Lookalike sites with near-identical names exist, so type the address yourself.

If a report has a mistake, dispute it free with the bureau and with the company that reported it. A late payment that really happened, and is recent, is different. No one can legally have it removed, so a company charging to erase one is selling nothing. Only time and on-time payments move a score past it.
