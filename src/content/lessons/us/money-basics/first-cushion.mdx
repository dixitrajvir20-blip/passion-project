## Six days before payday

Your phone screen cracks six days before payday. The repair is $180. Your account holds $60.

Nothing here is unusual. Each year the Federal Reserve asks adults across the country whether they could cover a $400 emergency expense with cash or its equivalent. In the survey it ran in October 2025, 63% said they could. The rest would need another way to cover it.

An <Term id="emergency-fund">emergency fund</Term>, or a cushion, is money set aside for exactly this kind of bill. **It is money for a bill you did not choose, kept apart from saving for something you want.**

## What the cushion is sized from

A cushion is sized from your must-pay costs, not from whatever is left at the end of the month. Must-pay costs arrive whether or not you are working: your share of the rent, your phone, getting to work, and food.

Say yours look like this.

| Must-pay cost | Per month |
| --- | --- |
| Rent share | $600 |
| Phone | $45 |
| Getting to work | $120 |
| Food | $290 |
| One month of must-pay costs | $1,055 |

Everything else is a want, and wants can shrink in a bad month. Sorting <Term id="needs-and-wants">needs and wants</Term> honestly is what makes the total useful. **A different rent or a longer commute makes a different number, so work out your own.**

## The first $400, then the first month

A $400 target is not a rule. It is the figure the Federal Reserve uses when it asks whether adults could meet an emergency expense, and it is small enough to reach.

At $50 from each paycheck, the eighth paycheck brings the cushion to $400. Paid every two weeks, that is about four months.

## What an overdraft costs instead

With no cushion, the $180 has to come from somewhere. Often that is the account itself. An <Term id="overdraft">overdraft</Term> happens when a payment is larger than your <Term id="available-balance">available balance</Term> and the bank pays it anyway. For that the bank charges an <Term id="overdraft-fee">overdraft fee</Term>.

Fee amounts differ from bank to bank, and so do the limits on how many can be charged. Take $35 an item as an example figure. Two small payments covered in the same week add $70 in fees. **The shortfall still has to be repaid, and the $70 is charged on top of it.**

## The opt-in rule on debit cards

Not every overdraft follows the same rule. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says that "banks cannot charge you an overdraft fee unless you opt in" for ATM withdrawals and one-time debit card purchases. Regulation E, the federal rule for electronic payments, sets the steps out: a written notice, a real chance to say yes, your consent, and a confirmation.

Checks and recurring electronic payments sit outside that rule. A bank may charge an overdraft fee on those whether or not you opted in.

**Whether you want that coverage is your decision, and you can change it.** Your bank can tell you what is set on the account now. Without an opt-in, a card is declined instead of covered.

## Paying yourself on payday

A target on its own has no date. Divide it by what you can move each payday, and a date appears.

What is left at the end of a month is decided by every spend before it, and some months that is nothing. Moving a fixed amount on payday puts the cushion first instead of last. Some people set a standing transfer for the day after pay lands. Others move it by hand and mark it off.

Plan from <Term id="net-pay">net pay</Term>, the figure that reaches the account, not from the hourly rate. **Spending the cushion on an unplanned bill is the cushion doing its job.** Then the same amount starts again, from the next payday.
