## Three numbers, not one

An <Term id="aid-offer">aid offer</Term> lists grants, scholarships, work-study and loans on one page, in one column. They are not the same kind of money. A grant is not a bill. **A loan comes back as a payment, every month, for years.**

So read a loan as three numbers. The <Term id="principal">principal</Term> is what you borrow. The <Term id="interest">interest</Term> rate is what borrowing costs each year, charged on what you still owe. The <Term id="loan-term">loan term</Term> is how many months you take to repay.

Four years of $5,000 loans is $20,000 of principal. At 6.52% over ten years, that is about $227 a month, 120 times.

## Grants and scholarships come first

The <Term id="fafsa">FAFSA</Term> is the free federal aid form. Filing it is what puts you in line for federal grants, work-study and loans.

Two kinds of money then sit on the same page. Grants and scholarships generally stay with you. The CFPB names one common exception: leave school part-way through a year and part of a grant can be owed back.

A <Term id="pell-grant">Pell Grant</Term> is one of the most common federal grants. Awards from colleges, employers and charities work the same way.

For the 2026-27 school year the federal FAFSA deadline is June 30, 2027. States and colleges set their own dates. **Settle the money that is not repaid before you decide how much to borrow.**

## From a total to a monthly bill

The payment, not the total, is the part you live with. At 6.52% a $20,000 loan runs about $227 a month for ten years. Stretch the same loan to twenty years and the payment drops to about $149.

**A smaller payment means owing for longer, and paying more interest in total.** Neither length suits everyone. The question is which payment fits the month, and what that fit costs.

## Why the federal rate is the same for everyone

Federal student loan rates are set once a year by a formula in law. Undergraduate Direct Loans first paid out from July 1, 2026 to June 30, 2027 carry a fixed 6.52%. Federal Student Aid worked it from the 10-year Treasury note auction before June 1, plus a fixed add-on.

Fixed means it holds for the life of that loan. Each July's loans get their own rate, so four years of borrowing can hold four different rates.

A private loan is priced differently. The CFPB says one may carry a <Term id="variable-rate">variable rate</Term> that resets every month or quarter, so the payment moves. It also calls federal loans the better option for the vast majority of borrowers.

## Interest can start before the first payment

Payments on most federal loans begin after a <Term id="grace-period">grace period</Term>. The CFPB says that is six months on Direct Loans, counted from when you graduate, leave school, or drop below half-time.

Interest often starts long before that. On a <Term id="subsidized-loan">subsidized loan</Term>, the government pays the interest while you are enrolled. On an <Term id="unsubsidized-loan">unsubsidized loan</Term>, and on private loans, it builds from the day the money is paid out.

At 6.52%, $5,000 builds about $326 of interest a year, or about $27 a month. **No bill is due yet, and the amount owed is growing.** Unpaid interest can later be added to the principal, which the CFPB calls <Term id="capitalization">capitalization</Term>.

## What changed on July 1, 2026

Loans first paid out on or after that date come with a different set of <Term id="repayment-plan">repayment plans</Term>. The Department of Education describes the Repayment Assistance Plan as 1% to 10% of income, less $50 a month for each dependent. The other route is a fixed term of 10, 15, 20 or 25 years, set by the amount borrowed.

The 2026 law behind those plans left undergraduate borrowing limits alone. It closed Grad PLUS to new borrowers, capped graduate borrowing at $20,500 a year and Parent PLUS at $20,000 a year per student, under a lifetime limit of $257,500.

## What a missed payment reaches

Federal repayment has room in it when income is low. The CFPB says to contact your loan servicer and go through the options, and to call before a payment is missed rather than after.

Missing payments is the expensive version. Missed federal student loan payments began appearing on credit reports again in the first quarter of 2025, once a reporting protection expired in October 2024. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York counted 2.2 million borrowers whose <Term id="credit-score">credit scores</Term> fell by more than 100 points in that quarter.

**Work out the payment before you sign, because that number stays with you for ten years or more.**
