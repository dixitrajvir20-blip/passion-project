## Three numbers, not one

An <Term id="aid-offer">aid offer</Term> lists grants, scholarships, work-study and loans on one page, in one column. They are not the same kind of money. A grant is not repaid. **A loan comes back as a bill, every month, for years.**

So read a loan as three numbers. The <Term id="principal">principal</Term> is what you borrow. The <Term id="interest">interest</Term> rate is what borrowing costs each year, charged on what you still owe. The <Term id="loan-term">loan term</Term> is how many months you take to repay.

Four years of $5,000 loans is $20,000 of principal. At 6.52% over ten years, that is about $227 a month, 120 times.

## Grants and scholarships come first

The <Term id="fafsa">FAFSA</Term> is the free federal aid form. Filing it is what puts you in line for federal grants, work-study and loans. USAGov puts the difference plainly: federal grants generally are not repaid, while a loan is money you pay back with interest.

A <Term id="pell-grant">Pell Grant</Term> is the largest of those grants. Scholarships from colleges, employers and charities work the same way, and that money never becomes a bill.

For the 2026-27 school year the federal FAFSA deadline is June 30, 2027. Colleges and states set their own, earlier dates. **Aid you keep is settled before aid you repay.**

## From a total to a monthly bill

The payment, not the total, is the part you live with. At 6.52% a $20,000 loan runs about $227 a month for ten years. Stretch the same loan to twenty years and the payment drops to about $149.

**A smaller payment means owing for longer, and more interest in total.** Neither length suits everyone. The question is which payment fits the month, and what that fit costs.

## Why the federal rate is the same for everyone

Federal student loan rates are set once a year, by a formula in law. For loans first paid out from July 1, 2026 to June 30, 2027, undergraduate Direct Loans carry a fixed 6.52%. Federal Student Aid worked it from the 10-year Treasury note auction held before June 1, plus a fixed add-on.

Fixed means it holds for the life of that loan. Each July's loans get their own rate, so four years of borrowing can hold four different rates.

A private lender prices by credit history instead, sometimes at a <Term id="variable-rate">variable rate</Term>. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says federal loans suit most borrowers, for the fixed rate and the wider protections.

## Interest can start before the first payment

Payments on federal loans start after you leave school and a <Term id="grace-period">grace period</Term>, usually six months. Interest often starts long before that.

On a <Term id="subsidized-loan">subsidized loan</Term>, the government covers the interest while you are enrolled. On an <Term id="unsubsidized-loan">unsubsidized loan</Term>, and on private loans, it builds from the day the money is paid out.

At 6.52%, $5,000 builds about $326 of interest a year, which is about $27 a month. **No bill is due yet, and the amount owed is growing.** Unpaid interest can later be added to the principal, which the CFPB calls <Term id="capitalization">capitalization</Term>. Interest is then charged on the larger balance.

## What changed on July 1, 2026

Loans first paid out on or after that date come with a different set of <Term id="repayment-plan">repayment plans</Term>. The Repayment Assistance Plan sets the payment between 1% and 10% of income, with $50 a month off for each dependent. The alternative is a fixed term of 10, 15, 20 or 25 years, longer for larger balances.

The same law left undergraduate borrowing limits alone. It closed Grad PLUS loans to new borrowers, capped graduate borrowing at $20,500 a year, and set a lifetime limit of $257,500. Parent PLUS borrowing is capped at $20,000 a year per student.

## A missed payment is not private

Federal repayment has room in it when income is low, and the plan can be changed by asking your loan servicer. That request costs nothing and is made before a payment is missed.

Missing payments is the expensive version. Student loan reporting to the credit bureaus restarted in 2025. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York counted more than 2.2 million borrowers whose <Term id="credit-score">credit scores</Term> fell by over 100 points in one quarter.

**Work out the payment before you sign, because that number stays with you for ten years or more.**
