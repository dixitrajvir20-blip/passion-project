## Two sides of every price

Two things push on every price. <Term id="supply">Supply</Term> is how much sellers have ready to sell. <Term id="demand">Demand</Term> is how much buyers want at each price.

When buyers want more than there is at today's price, that is a <Term id="shortage">shortage</Term>, and the price tends to rise. When sellers have more than buyers want, that is a <Term id="surplus">surplus</Term>, and the price tends to fall.

**So when a price jumps, ask first: which side moved?** Then ask a second question: how fast can sellers add more? The answer decides whether a jump lasts an hour or a month.

## Why gas costs more in summer

Gas prices follow a calendar. The Energy Information Administration (EIA), the government's energy statistics agency, says they tend to climb through spring and peak in late summer.

Both sides push the same way. On the demand side, people drive more in summer. On the supply side, summer gas costs more to make. Rules require a blend that evaporates less in the heat, and its ingredients cost refiners more.

Over the 20 years from 2004 to 2023, August gas averaged about 40 cents a gallon more than January gas. **A summer rise is the usual pattern, not a sign that something broke.**

## When a storm cuts supply

In late August 2017, Hurricane Harvey hit the Texas coast. Refineries are the plants that turn crude oil into gasoline, and the Gulf Coast has more refining capacity than the rest of the country put together. In the week after the storm, the region's refineries processed about a third less crude.

Drivers everywhere still needed as much gas as before. EIA's weekly survey shows the national average for regular rose 28 cents in one week, from $2.40 to $2.68 a gallon.

Refineries came back step by step through September and October. **After two weeks near the top, the price eased week by week.** A jump caused by short supply lasts about as long as the supply stays short.

## Seats that cannot grow

An arena holds a set number of seats. When a big artist announces a tour, far more fans may want tickets than there are seats. That shortage shows up as a sell-out in minutes, a long online queue, and resale listings far above face value.

Some sellers now use <Term id="dynamic-pricing">dynamic pricing</Term>, raising the price of the seats left as demand shows itself. **When supply cannot grow, extra demand lands on the price.**

Two habits help. Since May 2025, a federal rule says the first ticket price you see must include the required fees. Tax and delivery can be listed separately. Compare those totals, and set your top price before the sale opens. Resale rules vary by state.

## Surge pricing: a price by the minute

Many ride apps change prices minute by minute. When a stadium empties, thousands of people ask for a ride at once. Only so many drivers are nearby, so the price climbs.

The high price does two jobs. It asks riders who can wait to wait, and it pulls more drivers toward the crowd. **Both push the price back down as the crowd thins.**

So a surge price describes a moment, not the whole night. If you can wait somewhere safe, checking again later may cost less. If you need to get home now, paying more is a fair trade.
