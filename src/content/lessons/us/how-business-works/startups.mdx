## Two kinds of outside money

Savings and early sales are the first money most businesses have. When they are not enough, outside money comes in two kinds.

A loan is borrowed. You pay it back plus interest, the price of borrowing, every month, slow months included. Then it ends, and you still own all of the business.

A share is sold. Someone pays for <Term id="equity">equity</Term>, which means part of the business itself. Nothing is paid back and no bill arrives each month. **But the slice stays theirs, with its part of every profit and every sale.**

| | Loan | Share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paid back? | Yes, with interest | No |
| A bill every month? | Yes | No |
| When does it end? | After the last payment | Only if the business buys it back |
| If the business closes | What is left is still owed | The share is worth nothing |

## Your own money first

Paying for a start with savings and early sales is called <Term id="bootstrapping">bootstrapping</Term>. Nobody else owns a slice or gets a say, and nothing is owed. The catch, as the Small Business Administration (SBA) points out, is that any loss is yours alone.

It is also slower. The T-shirt business could buy a smaller press now and the big one after more club orders. Asking each club for a <Term id="order-deposit">deposit</Term> when it orders brings that day closer.

Some sellers use rewards crowdfunding. Backers pay now and get the product or a perk later. They are buying a product, not a slice of the business.

**Growing only as fast as sales allow is a real choice, not a failure.**

## Family and friends

Often the first outside money comes from people who know the founder. The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) oversees investing. It puts a typical friends-and-family round at about $10,000 to $50,000, as a loan or a share.

**The risk is not only the money. It is two people remembering two different deals.** An offer of 20%, with the details sorted out later, leaves every hard question open.

So settle it in writing before any money moves. Is it a gift, a loan or a share? A loan needs an amount, a monthly payment and an end date. A share needs to say what it covers, what say comes with it, and what happens if the business closes.

## Loans, and what the SBA does

Banks and other lenders make business loans. For its main business loans, the SBA does not lend the money itself. It promises the lender to cover part of the loss if a loan goes unpaid. That is a <Term id="loan-guarantee">loan guarantee</Term>, and it makes lenders more willing to say yes.

**The guarantee protects the lender, not you. Every payment is still yours to make.**

The lender decides who gets a loan and sets the terms. Expect to show a business plan, your costs and the sales you expect. SBA microloans go up to $50,000, through nonprofit community lenders, over up to seven years. The average is about $13,000. This lesson does not recommend any lender or loan.

## Angels and venture capital

An <Term id="angel-investor">angel investor</Term> is a wealthy person who puts their own money into young companies for a share. Many have built companies themselves. The SEC notes that angels often join as a director or an adviser.

<Term id="venture-capital">Venture capital</Term> funds invest money pooled from others in companies that could grow very large, very fast. A fund usually runs for ten years or more. It hopes to sell each share later for far more than it paid. Most expect a seat on the board, the small group that votes on big decisions.

**So investors buy a say as well as a slice.** A steady local business is rarely what a fund looks for, and that says nothing bad about the business.

## What 20% really means

Your aunt's $4,000 for 20% puts a <Term id="valuation">valuation</Term> of $20,000 on the whole business. That is what her offer says it is worth today.

Where does her 20% come from? The business issues new shares to her, so every existing slice shrinks by a fifth. You go from 60% to 48%, and Jordan from 40% to 32%. That shrinking is <Term id="dilution">dilution</Term>.

Her 20% is a fifth of any profit paid out and of any sale, for as long as she keeps it. **If the business closes with nothing left, her $4,000 is lost and nobody owes it back. With a loan, the debt would still be there.**

## Before anyone signs

Many young businesses close. Of the US business sites that were new in March 2015, about half were still open five years later, the Bureau of Labor Statistics found. So a share can end up worth nothing. That risk is why investors ask for large slices.

Selling shares also falls under federal securities law, whoever the buyer is. The SEC makes no exception for family and friends. Its small-business pages explain the rules, and a lawyer should check any deal before it is signed.

Under 18, what you can sign on your own varies by state. Bring in a parent or guardian before you agree to anything.
