## The top line

An <Term id="income-statement">income statement</Term> covers one stretch of time, such as a month. It asks three questions, in order. What came in? What did it cost? What was left? You may also see it called a profit and loss statement, or P&L.

The first line is <Term id="revenue">revenue</Term>, sometimes labeled sales. It is everything customers paid. In July the truck sold 1,000 plates at $12 each, so revenue was $12,000.

**That is where the counting starts, not what the truck made.** Every line below it takes something away.

## Gross profit: what each plate leaves

Every plate needs tortillas, meat, onions, salsa and a paper tray. That comes to about $4 a plate. Added up across the month, those costs are the <Term id="cost-of-goods-sold">cost of goods sold</Term>: $4,000 for July. Some statements call it cost of sales.

Take that from revenue and you get <Term id="gross-profit">gross profit</Term>, $8,000 in July. It is a test of the product. **Once a plate has paid for its own food, is anything left over?** Here each plate keeps $8, so yes.

## Net profit: after every bill

Next come the costs of running the truck at all. The kitchen she rents to prep food, and the spot where the truck parks at night. The truck lease. Wages for weekend helpers, including you. Propane, generator fuel, insurance and permits. Together these are <Term id="operating-expenses">operating expenses</Term>: $6,400 for July.

Take them from gross profit and you reach <Term id="net-profit">net profit</Term>, the bottom line. **Out of $12,000 of sales, July left $1,600.**

## Where the $1,600 comes from

Now read the month one plate at a time. Each plate keeps $8, but early in the month that $8 is not profit yet. It goes toward the $6,400 of operating expenses, which are owed however many plates sell.

**Only once those bills are covered does a plate add to the bottom line.** July sold enough to clear them, with plates to spare. The worked example below finds the exact plate where the month turns.

That is also why a slow week costs more than it seems. The bills stay the same, so fewer plates are left over after them.

## Profit is the owner's pay

Your aunt is the truck's only owner, and she pays herself no wage. The $2,400 of wages went to helpers. So the $1,600 at the bottom is her pay for the month, before tax. Money she takes out for herself is an <Term id="owners-draw">owner's draw</Term>. It comes out of profit and is never a cost above it.

The IRS form for a one-owner business, <Term id="schedule-c">Schedule C</Term>, reads the same way. It runs from gross receipts through cost of goods sold and expenses to net profit or loss. Its wages line leaves out anything owners pay themselves.

**Once net earnings from working for yourself reach $400 in a year, <Term id="self-employment-tax">self-employment tax</Term> applies.** Take tax questions to the IRS.

## What the statement does not show

This statement counts a sale on the day the food is served. Say an office orders 40 plates for lunch on July 31 and pays two weeks later. That $480 is in July's revenue, but not yet in the bank. The gap between profit on paper and money in the account is <Term id="cash-flow">cash flow</Term>.

**The statement says what a month earned. The bank balance says what has arrived.** Check both before spending profit.

Keep a record of every sale and every cost, with the receipts. The IRS notes that good records show how a business is doing and back up what goes on a tax return.
