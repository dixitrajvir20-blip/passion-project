## Where $20 goes

You print your own designs on shirts and sell them on an online marketplace for $20 each. A blank shirt, the ink and a padded mailer cost $12. The buyer pays for shipping on top.

On paper you keep $8. But the marketplace takes 10% of every sale, before the money reaches you. That cut is a <Term id="platform-fee">platform fee</Term>, and on a $20 shirt it is $2.

So each shirt leaves you $6, not $8. That $6 is your real <Term id="margin">margin</Term>. The fee is a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: every shirt you sell pays it again. If a platform charges more than one fee per sale, add them all.

## Ten percent of what?

It is easy to read 10% as a tenth of your profit. It is not. **A percentage fee is worked out on the sale price, not on what you keep.**

Ten percent of $20 is $2. That $2 comes out of your $8, and there it is a quarter. So the rate on the fee page is always smaller than the share it takes from you.

Your month has costs too. You pay back $100 a month for a heat press, and $20 for a design app. Those <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term> come to $120 whatever you sell.

Without the fee, 15 shirts would cover the month. With it, you need 20. That 20th shirt is your <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point.

## Same fee, different bite

A 10% fee does not hit every seller the same way. What matters is the gap between price and cost.

Say a friend pours candles. They sell for $20 and cost $4 to make, so she keeps $16 before the fee. The $2 fee takes an eighth of it.

Now say a cousin resells used game consoles. He buys one for $90 and sells it for $100. His gap is $10, and 10% of $100 is also $10. **The fee takes all of it.**

That is why a percentage fee weighs most on resellers, and on anyone selling on a thin margin. Set a platform's fee against your margin, not against your price.

## A month on the marketplace

Now look at a whole month. Say you sell 30 shirts and spend 20 hours designing, printing and packing.

Sales come to $600. Blanks, ink and mailers take $360, and the fee takes $60. After the $120 of monthly costs, $60 is left for you. That is $3 for each hour you worked.

**At this size, the marketplace keeps as much from your shirts as you do.** Without the fee, you would keep $120, or $6 an hour.

Sell 60 shirts, and the picture changes. The fee grows to $120, but the monthly costs stay at $120. You keep $240.

## What the fee pays for

A fee is not a penalty. It is the price of what the platform brings: buyers who would never find you, card payments handled for you, and a checkout people trust.

Selling in person has no cut, but it has its own costs. A table at a school fair may cost a flat amount, and fewer buyers walk past. So the useful question is not whether a fee is high. **It is whether the sales a platform brings cover what it takes.**

Keep a record of each month's fees too. If a <Term id="form-1099-k">Form 1099-K</Term> ever arrives, the IRS says its amount is before fees. Fees can be taken off it, and your record shows them.
