## Where the $80 goes

The bakery agrees $80 for the logo. The platform keeps 20% of that, so $16 never reaches you. That cut is a <Term id="platform-fee">platform fee</Term>.

A licensed font for this job costs $6. What is left is $58.

The client's side is not $80 either. The platform charges the bakery $4 on top, so the bakery pays $84 for work that pays you $64. Every fee rate in this lesson is an example; each platform sets its own.

## A percentage of the price, not the profit

It is easy to read 20% as a fifth of your profit. It is not. **A percentage fee is worked out on the price, not on what you keep.**

Twenty percent of $80 is $16. Set that beside the $58 you keep, and it is about 28% of it.

The fee is a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: every job you take pays it again. Your <Term id="margin">margin</Term> is what is left once the fee and the font are paid.

The thinner that margin, the harder the same rate bites. On a job that mostly pays for materials, a fifth of the price can be most of what you keep.

## The month behind one job

Your month costs $290 whether a job comes in or not: a laptop you are paying off, design software and your portfolio site. Those are <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>.

Each $80 job puts $58 towards them, so five jobs cover the month. The fifth job is your <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point.

Without the platform's cut, four would do it. **The fee costs you one extra job a month.**

## What the fee buys

A fee is not a penalty. It is the price of what the platform brings.

Here it brings the bakery, a client who would not have found you, and holds the bakery's money in <Term id="escrow">escrow</Term> until the logo is delivered. The job is written down as a <Term id="scope-of-work">scope of work</Term> before it starts, so both sides know what $80 covers. Platforms differ; check what yours covers.

Finding your own clients costs nothing in fees. It costs unpaid hours, and the risk of an invoice nobody pays.

The useful question is not whether 20% is high. **It is whether what a platform brings is worth what it takes.**

## When a client asks to pay you directly

Sooner or later a client suggests paying outside the platform and splitting the fee. From both sides it looks like a saving.

What it removes is the part you were paying for. Nobody holds the money until you deliver. There is no record of what was agreed, and no route to take if the file is refused or the payment never arrives.

The terms you agreed to when you joined may cover this too. Read them before you answer.

## Find the fees before you sign up

Fees are easier to price in before you start.

The FTC's guidance for anyone taking on gig work is to research the platform first. Find out how it pays, what charges can be taken out of your pay, and which of those you can avoid. It also suggests asking people already working there what they earn and what it costs them.

Then keep your own record of what each job was charged. The IRS says the total a platform reports on a <Term id="form-1099-k">Form 1099-K</Term> is not reduced by its fees, and that fees can be deducted from it. Your record is what supports that.
