## The message you did not ask for

A job scam starts where a real job rarely does: an unexpected text, or a message on WhatsApp or Telegram. The pay is high, the interview is short or missing, and the work stays vague.

Adults aged 18 to 59 reported losing money to job scams at more than five times the rate of people 60 and over. That comes from an FTC analysis of reports filed in 2021.

**A real employer pays you. It never needs a deposit from you, a check banked on its behalf, or money forwarded on.** Every screen below is a way around that one line.

## The check that has not cleared

A bank has to make deposited funds available within days. Available is a rule about timing. It is not a verdict on the check.

A <Term id="fake-check">fake check</Term> can take weeks to be found out. When it is, the bank takes back the whole amount. Anything you already sent on was your own money, so you are left owing the rest.

**A rising <Term id="available-balance">available balance</Term> tells you nothing about the check.** Ask your bank when the check has truly settled, and send nothing until it has. An employer that mails you a check to bank and pass on has no honest reason to.

## A Social Security number is a hiring step

Payroll does need it. It needs it after you are hired, on a <Term id="w-4">W-4</Term> and a Form I-9, from an employer with a name and an address you can check.

A chat asking for it before any offer is not payroll. It is the start of <Term id="identity-theft">identity theft</Term>: accounts and credit taken out in your name, which can take years to clear.

**Ask for the offer in writing first.** An offer names the employer, the job, the pay and the start date. The forms come after it. If a recruiter will not put that in writing, there is nothing to fill in yet.

## Reshipping and forwarded money

A <Term id="reshipping">reshipping</Term> job asks you to take in parcels at home, strip the receipts and post them on under your own name. The goods are usually bought with stolen cards, and the trail ends at your address. The FTC is plain that this is not real work.

Forwarding money is the same trick with a bank account instead. Anyone who moves stolen money through an account in their own name is a <Term id="money-mule">money mule</Term>, and that name is the one a bank can find.

**No job needs your account as a staging post.** Pay at a real job moves one way.

## Task apps and gift cards

A <Term id="task-scam">task scam</Term> shows earnings that climb as you click, then asks for a deposit of your own money before anything can be taken out. Small payments of $5 to $20 arrive early, and that is what makes the rest look real. The deposit releases nothing.

Gift cards are the other common demand, often in a text from a manager with a new number. "No real business or government agency will ever tell you to buy a gift card to pay them," the FTC says.

**Codes texted to someone are spent in minutes.** Check any such request on a number you already have.

## If money has already gone

Speed helps, and so does the record.

Tell your bank the same day if a check was deposited or a transfer sent. Then report it at reportfraud.ftc.gov, whether or not you lost anything. A report with no loss still counts, and it is how a live scam gets tracked.

Keep what you have: the messages, the account or number you sent to, the amounts and the dates. Screenshots are enough.

**Being caught by one of these says nothing about you.** The scripts are written, tested and run at scale, and they work on careful people too.
