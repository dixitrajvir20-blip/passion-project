## The message you did not ask for

A job scam starts where a real job rarely does: an unexpected text, or a message on WhatsApp or Telegram. The pay is high, the interview is short or missing, and the work stays vague.

Adults aged 18 to 59 reported losing money on job scams at more than five times the rate of people 60 and over. That comes from an FTC analysis of reports filed in 2021.

**A real employer pays you. It never needs a deposit from you, a check banked on its behalf, or money forwarded on.** Every screen below is a way around that one line.

## The check that has not cleared

Your bank has to release deposited funds quickly. That deadline is about timing. It says nothing about whether the check is good.

Weeks can pass before a <Term id="fake-check">fake check</Term> is found to be forged. When it is, the bank wants the whole amount back from you. Anything you already sent on was your own money, so you are left owing the rest.

**A rising <Term id="available-balance">available balance</Term> tells you nothing about the check.** Ask your bank when the check has truly settled, and send nothing until it has. An employer that mails you a check to bank and pass on has no honest reason to.

## A Social Security number is a hiring step

Payroll does need it, once you work there. An employer has to put your name and number on a <Term id="w-2">W-2</Term>, asks for a signed <Term id="w-4">W-4</Term> when you start, and completes a <Term id="form-i-9">Form I-9</Term> for every hire.

A chat asking for it before any offer is not payroll. It is a way into <Term id="identity-theft">identity theft</Term>. The FTC describes that as someone using your details without permission to open accounts, get medical care, or get a job in your name.

**Ask for the offer in writing first.** An offer names the employer, the job, the pay and the start date. The forms come after it. If a recruiter will not put that in writing, there is nothing to fill in yet.

## Reshipping and forwarded money

A <Term id="reshipping">reshipping</Term> job asks you to take in parcels at home, strip the receipts and post them on under your own name. The FTC says the goods are often electronics bought with stolen cards, and that this is never a real job.

Forwarding money is the same shape with a bank account instead. Someone who moves stolen money for a scammer is what law enforcement calls a money mule. The FTC warns that this can mean legal trouble, even for someone who did not know.

**No job needs your bank account to pass money on.** Pay at a real job moves one way, towards you.

## Task apps and gift cards

A <Term id="task-scam">task scam</Term> shows earnings that climb as you click, then asks for a deposit of your own money before anything can be taken out. A few dollars do arrive early. The FTC puts them at $5 to $20, and that makes the rest look real. The deposit releases nothing.

Gift cards are the other common demand, often in a text from a manager's new number. "No real business or government agency will ever tell you to buy a gift card to pay them," the FTC says.

**The numbers on the back are the money.** Whoever has them can spend the card, and the money is rarely recovered. Check any such request on a number you already have.

## If money has already gone

Two things matter here: moving quickly, and keeping a record.

Tell your bank the same day if you deposited a check or sent a transfer. Then report it at reportfraud.ftc.gov. The FTC asks people to report scams they see, not only the ones that cost money.

Keep the messages, the amounts, the dates and any account or number you sent money to. Screenshots will do.

**Being caught by one of these says nothing about you.** These scripts are written once and run at volume, and careful people are caught by them too.
