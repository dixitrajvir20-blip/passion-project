## Four payments is credit

At checkout a button splits $60 into four payments of $15. The seller is still paid in full today. A lender pays it, and you pay the lender back. That is <Term id="buy-now-pay-later">buy now, pay later</Term>, or pay-in-four. The CFPB describes the common plan as four interest-free payments, two weeks apart.

The price does not change. Splitting changes when you pay, not how much. **After the payment made at checkout, $45 of that $60 is still owed.**

Nearly every borrower in the CFPB's survey paid by <Term id="autopay">autopay</Term>, from a debit card or a bank account. Each installment then leaves on its date by itself, whether or not the money is there.

## Who pays the plan

A lender that charges you no interest still earns, and most of it comes from the other side of the sale. The seller pays a <Term id="merchant-discount-fee">merchant discount fee</Term>, a cut of each sale the plan brings in.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) surveyed five large lenders. Across all of them in 2021, seller fees came to 2.49% of everything bought through the plans, and late fees to 0.28%. One seller's own rate is set in its contract.

**A price has to cover that cut, the way it covers every other cost of selling.** What the plan sells the seller is bigger orders: its pitch to retailers, quoted in that report, claims higher average order values and more completed checkouts.

## Three plans, one payday

One plan is simple to track. Three running at once is harder, and it is common. Looking at six large lenders, the CFPB found that about 63% of their borrowers had more than one loan running at the same time during 2022. That is <Term id="loan-stacking">loan stacking</Term>, the CFPB's own name for it.

In its 2022 market report, the CFPB found lenders have limited sight of a borrower's plans on other platforms. Your <Term id="credit-report">credit report</Term> may not close that gap either, because payment history is not always reported.

**So you are the only one who can add every installment against one payday.** Three $60 purchases in a week make twelve installments of $15, spread across six weeks.

## What a missed installment costs

A 0% plan stays free only while every installment clears. When one fails, the lender's own terms decide what follows, and those differ.

Most plans charge a <Term id="late-fee">late fee</Term>. In the CFPB's report it was often around $7 on an average loan of $135, and 10.5% of borrowers were charged at least one during 2021. One lender's policy allowed more than one fee for a single missed installment. Your bank can add an <Term id="overdraft-fee">overdraft fee</Term> on top.

**Ask before the date, not after.** The CFPB found several lenders waive a late fee when a borrower asks or shows hardship. An unpaid loan can be turned over to a <Term id="debt-collector">debt collector</Term> and reported to a credit bureau.

## Add it up before the next checkout

Three things to check, all of them before the button.

First, the installments already running: what each takes, and on which date. Second, which paycheck each date falls in, because pay every two weeks and installments every two weeks do not always line up. Third, what is left of that paycheck once rent and food have their share.

<Term id="cash-flow">Cash flow</Term> is the name for that timing. Money earned is not the same as money in the account on a Friday. **A plan is a loan, so it belongs on the list with everything else you owe.**
