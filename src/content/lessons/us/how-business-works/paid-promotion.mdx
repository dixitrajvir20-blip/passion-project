## The post and the link

The account posts a screenshot of the week's gains, then a link to a trading app. The caption says 20% a month. Under it, hundreds of comments ask how to start.

Two questions sort a post like this. Who is paid when you tap? And is that person allowed to be paid for this?

**Neither question is about whether the tip is right.** You cannot check that from a feed. Both of these you can check in a few minutes.

The FTC's own fraud reports show why it is worth the few minutes. People aged 18 to 59 report losing money to an investment scam over four times as often as people 60 and over.

## Payment is not only money

The FTC calls it a <Term id="material-connection">material connection</Term>: any financial, employment, personal or family relationship with the brand being mentioned. Where one exists, the post has to say so, in words people will see and understand.

Free product counts. So do perks, a free account and a discount.

A <Term id="referral-link">referral link</Term> counts too. The creator is paid for each account opened through it. That is payment, even though nothing changes hands on screen.

**A line saying "not financial advice" does not cover any of this.** It is about advice, not about who paid.

## Paid to publicize a stock

Securities law has its own version of the rule, and it is older. In 2017 the SEC charged 27 people and firms behind stock-promotion schemes, where paid articles read as independent analysis.

"If a company pays someone to publish or publicize articles about its stock, it must be disclosed," the SEC said when it announced the cases.

**Independent and paid are the two things a reader cannot tell apart without the label.** That is what the label is for.

## Who is allowed to be paid for advice

Someone paid to advise other people about securities has to be registered. That means an <Term id="investment-adviser">investment adviser</Term> with the SEC or a state, or a broker through FINRA.

<Term id="brokercheck">BrokerCheck</Term> is FINRA's free register. It shows whether a person or firm is registered, which licences they hold, and any complaints or disciplinary events on the record. Advisers registered with the SEC rather than FINRA come up from the same search.

FINRA also warns that some people falsely claim to be registered, sometimes using a real professional's name. **So search the register yourself rather than reading a screenshot.**

## Twenty percent a month, twelve times

Take the caption at its word for a moment. Twenty percent a month is nowhere near 20% a year, because each month is worked out on the total the month before left.

FINRA puts words like guaranteed and risk-free under phantom riches, a persuasion tactic, because no investment can offer them.

## When a betting app uses trading words

Some betting apps borrow the vocabulary. Picks are called trades, a balance is called a portfolio, and a deposit bonus stands in where growth would be.

The words are not the difference. A wager pays out only if one named event goes one way, and the stake is gone if it does not. Nothing is left afterwards that can grow. The legal age to bet varies by state.

This is not a rare corner of the feed. In an NCAA survey of 3,527 people aged 18 to 22, published in May 2023, 58% had taken part in at least one sports betting activity.

## If money has already gone

Report it. Reports are how the FTC sees a pattern across thousands of people and brings cases against the operators.

Its 2025 data shows the size of this. People reported losing $2.1 billion to scams that started on social media, and $1.1 billion of that to investment scams.

**An account that shows a balance, then asks for a fee to release it, is charging you twice.** The balance is a number the group controls.
