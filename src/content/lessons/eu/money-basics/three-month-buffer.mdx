## The month with no income

Across the EU, 49% of adults aged 18 to 65 do not have enough savings to cover three months of living costs. The European Commission published that figure in September 2025.

The Commission's 2023 survey shows the gap is widest for the youngest adults. Only 17% of 18 to 24 year olds could cover six months or more, against 40% of people aged 55 and over.

Your flatmate has no buffer. The rent share is due on the 1st, and the gap gets filled with debt: an <Term id="overdraft">overdraft</Term>, instalments at the checkout, or a loan from a friend.

An <Term id="emergency-fund">emergency fund</Term>, or buffer, is money kept for that month. **Its job is to pay the essentials when income stops.**

## Three months of what

The target is three months of essentials, not three months of pay. Essentials are the costs that arrive whether or not you are earning: your rent share, transport, your phone and food. The rest of your <Term id="needs-and-wants">needs and wants</Term> can wait or shrink in a bad month.

Say your <Term id="net-pay">net pay</Term> is €1,150 a month and your essentials come to €730. Three months of pay would be €3,450. Three months of essentials is €2,190, and that is the target.

| Essential | Per month |
| --- | --- |
| Rent share | €400 |
| Transport pass | €60 |
| Phone | €20 |
| Food | €250 |
| One month of essentials | €730 |
| Three months of essentials | €2,190 |

**The number is yours, and it moves with you.** Rent differs between cities and countries. Poland, Sweden and Czechia have their own currencies, so there the target is in złoty, kronor or koruna.

## A monthly amount and a date

A target of €2,190 on its own has no date. Divide it by the months you give it, and it becomes a monthly amount and a date. Twelve months means about €185 a month. At €100 a month it takes 22 months. At €250 a month, nine.

**Move the amount on payday, before the first spend, so saving is not whatever is left over.** Left over is decided by every purchase before it, and some months it is nothing.

One month of essentials, €730, is a useful first target on the way to three.

## Why the rate is not the plan

The calculation above uses a rate of 0% on purpose. It shows what the monthly amount does by itself: €185 a month is past €2,190 inside a year, with no help from <Term id="interest">interest</Term>.

Whatever an account pays comes on top, and at the rates a buffer usually earns, it is small. In the practice step below, an example 1% over two years adds less than one month's deposit.

Prices rise too. A low rate may not keep up with <Term id="inflation">inflation</Term>, which the lesson on inflation takes further. For this money, that is the price of knowing it is there. **The rate decides very little here. The amount and the months decide the date.**

## Where it waits

Two things matter about where a buffer waits. You can reach the money within a day or two, which is <Term id="liquidity">liquidity</Term>, and the amount does not rise and fall while it sits there. Keeping it apart from spending money makes it harder to spend by accident.

**Where exactly it waits is your decision.** This lesson describes what is protected and what is not, and leaves the choice with you.

## What the guarantee covers

In every EU country, money in a bank account is covered by a <Term id="deposit-guarantee">deposit guarantee</Term>. If the bank fails, the national scheme pays it back. The limit is €100,000 for each person at each bank, or that figure converted into the national currency. Each country runs its own scheme under one EU rule.

Company shares, <Term id="investment-fund">investment funds</Term> and <Term id="crypto-asset">crypto-assets</Term> are not bank deposits. Their value can fall on the day you need the money, and the guarantee does not cover a fall in value. If the place holding your money is not a bank, ask how it is protected, and check with your national scheme or financial regulator.
