## Two per cent, five times over

<Term id="interest">Interest</Term> on savings is worked out on the balance in the account. In the first year that balance is the €100 you put in, the <Term id="principal">principal</Term>, so the interest is €2.

In the second year the balance is €102, and 2% of €102 is €2.04. The extra four cents is interest on the first year's interest. That is <Term id="compound-growth">compound growth</Term>.

**Five years at 2% is €100 multiplied by 1.02 five times, not €2 added five times.** The gap is small at this size, and it widens with every year the money stays.

| After | 2% on the balance each year | 2% on the first €100 only |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Year 1 | €102.00 | €102 |
| Year 2 | €104.04 | €104 |
| Year 3 | €106.12 | €106 |
| Year 4 | €108.24 | €108 |
| Year 5 | €110.41 | €110 |

## The rate in a sum is an assumption

A worked example needs a rate to multiply by. The 2% used here is chosen so that the arithmetic can be shown. It is not an offer from anyone, and it is not a forecast.

What an account pays is set out in that account's own terms, including whether the rate is fixed for a period or can change. Read those terms before trusting a table of future amounts.

**Use a rate to see how a sum behaves, not to predict what an account will pay you.** Where your savings sit is your decision, and this lesson does not make it for you.

## What the HICP measures

Prices for the things households buy tend to rise over time. That is <Term id="inflation">inflation</Term>. Across the EU it is measured with the <Term id="hicp">HICP</Term>, a harmonised index that Eurostat publishes each month for every member state.

The European Central Bank is the <Term id="central-bank">central bank</Term> for the countries that use the euro. Since its 2021 strategy review it has aimed at 2% inflation over the medium term. It treats a miss below that as no more welcome than a miss above.

Each country has its own figure. In December 2025 the euro-area rate was 2.0%, Ireland was at 2.7% and France at 0.7%. Averaged over 2025, rates ran from 0.8% in Cyprus to 6.8% in Romania.

## The same 2% on the other side

The calculation above ends at about €3,318, from €3,000 of deposits over ten years. Now suppose prices rise 2% a year across those same ten years.

You would spend that €3,318 at tenth-year prices. Measured in today's prices, it buys about €2,722 worth. The deposits are future money too, paid across those same ten years. In today's prices the 120 payments come to about €2,719.

**Two per cent of growth against two per cent of inflation leaves buying power where it started.** The statement grew. What the money buys did not.

## Nominal and real, side by side

A balance is a <Term id="nominal-value">nominal value</Term>: euros counted, with no question asked about what they buy. Its <Term id="real-value">real value</Term> is what it buys.

A rough test fits on one line. Take the rate, subtract how fast prices are rising, and read the sign of what is left. Above zero, the savings buy more than before. Below zero, they buy less.

The same test works on a pay rise or a rent increase. **A balance that grows more slowly than prices buys less than it did.**
