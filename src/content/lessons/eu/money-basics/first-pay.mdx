## Gross, net and the gap between

Your contract names one number. Your account receives a smaller one. The first is <Term id="gross-pay">gross pay</Term>: what the job pays before anything comes off. The second is <Term id="net-pay">net pay</Term>, the money that actually lands.

The gap is not lost. Most of it is income tax and <Term id="social-contributions">social contributions</Term>, taken by the employer and passed on for you. Contributions pay for things like health cover, a future pension and support if you lose a job.

**Plan with net pay, because it is the only money you can spend.** The gross figure is still worth knowing. It is the number in job adverts, and the one a pay rise is measured against.

## Every country takes off something different

There is no EU-wide tax rate. Each member state sets its own income tax and runs its own social security. EU rules only link the systems, so work in one country can count in another. You also cannot choose: if you live and work in one country, its system normally covers you.

So the same wage can leave different amounts in two countries. In Germany, contributions for pension, health, long-term care and unemployment come straight out of pay. As a rule, the employer pays the same again on top. In Ireland, the employer takes income tax and the Universal Social Charge, using instructions from Revenue, the tax office.

**A friend's payslip from another country tells you little about yours.**

## Your first week, in three checks

Pay usually comes with a <Term id="payslip">payslip</Term>. Read it within the week, while you still remember the month.

First, check the hours or days and the rate against your own notes. Second, check your name and your tax or social security number, because the deductions are recorded against them. Third, find gross, each deduction and net, and see how one becomes the other.

Some countries add a task. In Ireland, a first job has to be registered with Revenue. Skip it and you may pay emergency tax, which usually takes more, until it is sorted.

**If a line makes no sense, ask the employer or your national tax authority.** It is a normal question, and they hear it often.

## Give each euro a job

Now the money. Before the first spend, split net pay into three jobs: needs, wants and savings. <Term id="needs-and-wants">Needs and wants</Term> are the first two. Needs come back every month: a rent share, a transport pass, a phone, food. Wants are what you choose: going out, clothes, subscriptions.

A common starting split is 50/30/20. That is half for needs, 30% for wants and 20% for savings. It is a guideline, not a rule.

**The percentages are yours to change.** Someone living at home may need far less than half for needs. Someone renting a room in a city may need more.

## Savings move on payday

The savings line works best when it moves the day the pay lands, before anything else. Saving whatever is left at the end of the month depends on something being left.

Its first job is an <Term id="emergency-fund">emergency fund</Term>, for the broken bike, the lost phone or the bill nobody warned you about. **Without that buffer, one surprise becomes debt, and next month starts short.**

Keep it somewhere you will not spend it by accident. Where exactly is your choice, and this lesson does not pick one for you. If you are paid in złoty, krona or forint, every step is the same.
