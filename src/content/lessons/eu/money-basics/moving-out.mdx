## The room, and the number it has to fit

€520 a month, plus bills. Your <Term id="net-pay">net pay</Term> is €1,125. The question is not whether €520 sounds like a lot. It is what €520 leaves.

Young people in the EU left the parental home at an average age of 26.2 in 2024. The average was 21.4 years in Finland and 31.3 years in Croatia. Whatever a room costs where you live, the test is the same one.

**That test is rent plus bills, measured against the pay that reaches your account.**

## What the 40% line measures

Eurostat, the EU's statistics office, has a marker for this. A household is in <Term id="housing-cost-overburden">housing cost overburden</Term> when total housing costs come to more than 40% of <Term id="disposable-income">disposable income</Term>.

Two details matter. The line is drawn on total housing costs, which take in water, electricity, gas and heating, not on the rent alone. And it describes a household, so on one income the sum is yours.

In 2024, 8.2% of people in the EU lived in an overburdened household. Among 15 to 29-year-olds it was 9.7%.

**The 40% line is a marker, not a rule.** It is where official statistics count housing as taking too large a share.

## Run it on this offer

Three figures go into the sum. Your net pay, €1,125. The rent, €520. And the bills, which the advert does not mention.

Ask the current tenants what the bills come to, and use their figure rather than an estimate. Take €80 for now. The same three figures work in any currency: in Poland the sum is in złoty, in Sweden in kronor.

**What decides this is the figure left after housing, not the rent on its own.** Here that figure is €525 a month.

## What the remainder has to cover

After housing, €525 covers food, transport, your phone and whatever breaks. It also has to leave something for an <Term id="emergency-fund">emergency fund</Term>.

Write that list out before you answer the landlord, using real figures from your own last three months.

**If the list does not fit, the room is not affordable.** A cheaper room, one more flatmate, or a few more months where you are: those are the levers you still hold. The rent, once the contract is signed, is not one of them.

## What day one costs

Rent is monthly. The move is not.

A <Term id="rental-deposit">rental deposit</Term> is money the landlord holds against unpaid rent or damage, and returns at the end if nothing is owed. Deposit rules are national: how large one may be, and how it must be held, differs between countries. Your national housing or consumer authority states the rule where you are renting.

Add the deposit, the first month and the cost of the move itself. **That total, not the monthly rent, is what has to fit inside your savings.**

## Never pay before you see it

The European Consumer Centre in Germany describes a steady pattern. The price sits far below the local market. The landlord is abroad and cannot arrange a viewing. Money is wanted by transfer, before any keys. Its advice is plain: "Never pay in advance, either for the deposit or for the keys".

**Pay nothing until you have seen the property and signed for it.** A <Term id="rental-contract">rental contract</Term> sets the rent, the notice period and who pays which bills.

Report a listing that asks for money up front to the portal operator. A <Term id="european-consumer-centre">European Consumer Centre</Term> advises free of charge when the business is in another EU country.
