## The number that did not move

Your balance counts euros. It does not tell you what those euros can buy. The first is the <Term id="nominal-value">nominal value</Term> of your money, and the second is its <Term id="real-value">real value</Term>.

When prices rise, each euro buys a little less. That is <Term id="inflation">inflation</Term>. **If prices rise and your balance stays the same, your savings shrink, even though the number never changes.**

Nothing is taken out of the account, so no app shows it. The change happens at the till: the same bag of shopping costs more euros than it did.

## Five years of euro-area prices

Eurostat measures prices across the euro area with the <Term id="hicp">HICP</Term>. It follows a basket of about 700 things households buy, from bread to bus fares to rent.

In 2021, 2024 and 2025, prices rose a little over 2%. In 2022 and 2023 they rose far faster: 8.4% and 5.4%. Taken together, prices were about 22.5% higher in 2025 than in 2020.

So the €1,000 that sat at 0% bought in 2025 what about €816 bought in 2020. **Those five years raised prices about as much as ten years at 2% would have.**

## The 2% the ECB aims for

The European Central Bank, or ECB, is the <Term id="central-bank">central bank</Term> for the countries that use the euro. It aims for inflation of 2% a year over the medium term, measured with the HICP. It treats a miss below 2% as just as unwelcome as a miss above it.

Why not 0%? One of the ECB's reasons is a safety margin. Prices falling across the whole economy are harder to deal with than a small rise.

Even at the target, money at 0% loses ground. After ten years at 2%, €1,000 buys what about €820 buys today.

The six EU countries outside the euro have their own central banks, with their own policies.

## Real and nominal, side by side

<Term id="interest">Interest</Term> is a nominal number too. Say an account paid 3% in 2023, when euro-area prices rose 5.4%. The balance grew, but its real value fell by a little over 2%.

A rough test: take the interest rate and subtract the rise in prices. Above zero, your savings buy more than before. Below zero, they buy less.

The same test works on a pay rise. **A number that grows is not the same as money that buys more.**

## Your prices, not the average

The HICP is an average. Your own prices can rise faster or slower, depending on what your money goes on. If most of it pays rent and a transport pass, those two prices matter most to you.

Countries differ too. In 2025, prices rose 0.9% in France and 6.8% in Romania, against 2.1% for the euro area. Your national statistics office publishes the figure for your country.

A low rate on an <Term id="emergency-fund">emergency fund</Term> may not keep up with prices. For money that has to be safe and within reach, that is the price of knowing it will be there. Where your savings wait is your choice, and this lesson does not pick a place for you.
