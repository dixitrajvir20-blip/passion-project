## From €1,500 to €1,150

Your contract names one figure. Your account receives a smaller one. The first is <Term id="gross-pay">gross pay</Term>: what the job pays before anything comes off. The second is <Term id="net-pay">net pay</Term>, the money that lands.

The €350 between them is not a fee the employer keeps. It is a set of <Term id="deduction">deductions</Term>, taken from your pay and sent on to the bodies they are owed to. Each one is named on your <Term id="payslip">payslip</Term>.

**Plan on net pay, because it is the only part you can spend.** The gross figure still matters. It is the number in job adverts, and the one a pay rise is measured against.

## What the contributions buy

<Term id="social-contributions">Social contributions</Term> are the larger line here. They pay into public systems: health cover, a pension, and support if you lose a job or cannot work.

Your employer pays into those same systems on top of your gross pay. Those <Term id="employer-contributions">employer contributions</Term> never reach your account, so the job costs more than your contract says.

Germany shows one way the two sides divide it. Staff and employer there put in the same amount, and the employer covers accident insurance on its own. Every country sets its own shares.

## What the job costs the employer

Eurostat measures both sides together as the <Term id="tax-wedge">tax wedge</Term>. It adds income tax to both lots of social contributions, then sets the total against the full cost of employing someone.

For a single person without children earning 67% of the average wage, the EU figure was 39.5% in 2025. Germany was highest, at 44.7%, and Ireland lowest, at 22.5%.

Countries differ that much, so the same gross figure can leave very different net pay. **A friend's payslip from another country tells you little about yours.**

## The tax line is an estimate

Where income tax comes off each payslip, it is worked out from an estimate of your whole year. If you start in September, or your hours drop, more can come off than you owe for the year.

What happens next depends on where you work. Ireland is one example. Once the year ends, an employee can ask Revenue for a statement of liability: a review of the year's tax. A refund follows if too much was paid.

**So the monthly figure is a running estimate, not a final bill.** Your national tax authority sets out how your year is settled.

## Check the payslip in the first week

Some countries want something from you first. In Ireland, you tell Revenue, the tax office, when you start your first job. Without that, your employer may have to use emergency tax, a stand-in basis used until Revenue can send them your details.

Then three checks, while the month is still fresh. The hours or days and the rate, against your own notes. Your name and your tax or social security number, because the deductions are recorded against them. Then gross, each deduction, and net.

**If a line makes no sense, ask the employer or your national tax authority.** It is an ordinary question.

## Plan the month on net

Net pay is €1,150, and that is the figure the month runs on. Before the first spend, give each euro a job: a rent share, transport, a phone, food, and the amount that moves to savings on payday.

What is left after that is not spare. It is the part you have not decided about yet.

The shares are yours to set. Someone living at home needs far less for rent than someone renting a room in a city. **Change the lines until they match your own month.**
