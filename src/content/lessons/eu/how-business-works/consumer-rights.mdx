## Two rights with different jobs

When a purchase goes wrong, two EU rights do most of the work, and people often mix them up. One is for changing your mind. The other is for things that break or do not work as advertised.

Both apply when you buy from a <Term id="trader">trader</Term>: a business, whether it is a shop in town or a website. **Buy from a private person and neither right applies.** On a second-hand app, check which one you are dealing with before you pay.

These are EU minimums, so they hold in all 27 countries, including the six that do not use the euro. Some countries give more, such as a longer guarantee. None can give less.

## Fourteen days to change your mind

Buy online, by phone or from a seller at your door, and you get 14 days to cancel. You do not need a reason. For goods, the days start when they arrive. This is the <Term id="right-of-withdrawal">right of withdrawal</Term>.

**Tell the seller within the 14 days, in writing, and keep a copy.** Sending the item back usually costs you the postage. The exceptions: the seller offered to pay, or did not tell you the cost before you bought.

In a shop, where you can see the item before paying, the law gives no such right. Many shops take returns anyway. That is their own policy, so read it before you pay.

## What the 14 days leave out

A few kinds of purchase have no change-of-mind right, even when bought online.

* Goods made to your own design, like a hoodie with your name printed on it.
* Food and flowers that go off quickly.
* Tickets and bookings for a fixed date, such as a concert, a train or a flight.
* Sealed music, films or software, once you open them.
* Digital downloads you started after agreeing to give up the right.

**An exception to the 14 days is not an exception to the guarantee.** If the printed hoodie falls apart in the first wash, that is a fault, and the seller still has to put it right.

## Two years if it breaks

If something is faulty or does not work as advertised, the <Term id="legal-guarantee">legal guarantee</Term> applies. It lasts at least two years from when you got the item, whether you bought it in a shop or online.

**The seller is responsible, not the maker.** The seller has to repair or replace it free, postage included. If neither works, or it takes too long, you get some or all of your money back.

In the first year, you do not have to prove the fault was there from the start. Some countries stretch that to two years. A store or maker's warranty is a <Term id="commercial-guarantee">commercial guarantee</Term>. It can add to the legal guarantee, never cut it short.

## Where to complain

Start with the seller, in writing. Say what is wrong, which right applies, and what you want: a repair, a replacement or a refund. Give a date to reply by. Keep the order confirmation, photos and every message.

If the seller is in another EU country, Iceland or Norway and will not help, contact the <Term id="european-consumer-centre">European Consumer Centre</Term> in the country where you live. **Its help is free.** A student in Kraków with a German online shop goes to the centre in Poland.

If the seller is in your own country, the next step is an out-of-court dispute service. Every EU country has these services, and they are low cost.
