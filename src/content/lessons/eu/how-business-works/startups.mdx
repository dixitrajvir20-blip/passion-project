## Four ways in

Every young company needs money before it earns much. The textbook app needs a server, a developer account and maybe a better laptop. That money can come from four places, and each one costs something different.

Your own savings and early sales cost nothing, but grow slowly. A grant is not paid back, but it comes with rules. A loan is paid back with <Term id="interest">interest</Term>. Investors pay for <Term id="equity">equity</Term>, which means part of the company itself.

**The question is never only how much. It is what this money costs.**

## Your own money first

Paying for a start from savings and early sales is called <Term id="bootstrapping">bootstrapping</Term>. Many small businesses begin this way. You grow only as fast as sales allow. But nobody else owns a slice, and nothing is owed.

It also tests the idea. **If people will not pay for a small version, a big version will not fix that.** Sell the first ten, then ask whether you need outside money for the next hundred.

## Grants: money with a job attached

A <Term id="grant">grant</Term> is not paid back. Instead, it pays for a stated project, with rules on what counts. You apply by a deadline, compete with others, and show how the money was spent.

Grants come from many levels. Each EU country manages part of the EU's funding on its own territory, so what exists differs by country and region. At the top end, the European Innovation Council gives grants under €2.5 million to firms with ideas too risky for private investors alone. It can also invest €1 million to €10 million.

**For a first project, start close to home.** Your country's <Term id="point-of-single-contact">point of single contact</Term> or regional business agency can say what exists where you live.

## Loans: paid back, whatever happens

A loan brings money now and a payment every month. You repay what you borrowed, plus interest. The payments are due in a slow month as much as a busy one.

The trade is simple, though. **Once the loan is repaid, it is over, and you still own all of the company.**

Much EU support for small firms reaches them through local banks and lenders, and those lenders decide who gets it. The European Commission lists EU-backed microloans of up to €25,000 for starting a small business. Rates and rules differ by lender and by country. The 7% in the calculator below is only an example, not an offer.

## Investors who buy a share

Some investors do not lend. They buy part of the company. A <Term id="business-angel">business angel</Term> is a private person, usually with business experience, who puts their own money into young companies. Many also bring advice and contacts, and they know a return may take years.

<Term id="venture-capital">Venture capital</Term> firms invest larger sums, often other people's money, in companies aiming to grow very fast. They plan to sell their share later for much more. A small, steady business is usually not what they look for, and that says nothing bad about it.

**Neither is paid back. That is the attraction, and also the cost.**

## What 20% really means

Selling equity means selling part of the company for good. The neighbour's offer of €20,000 for 20% puts a price of €100,000 on the whole app. You and your friend go from 50% each to 40% each.

Her 20% is a fifth of any profit shared out and a fifth of any sale, for as long as she holds it. Owners often get a say in big decisions too.

## Slices keep shrinking

Each new investor shrinks every existing slice again. That is <Term id="dilution">dilution</Term>. If a later investor takes 25%, your 40% becomes 30%.

**Most young companies are never sold, so a share can end up worth little.** That risk is why investors ask for a large slice. Under 18, ask your country's official business site what you can sign on your own. In złoty, koruna or krona, the shares work the same way.
