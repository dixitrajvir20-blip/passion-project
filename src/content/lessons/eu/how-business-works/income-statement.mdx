## Three lines tell the story

An <Term id="income-statement">income statement</Term> covers one stretch of time, such as a month. It answers three questions in order. What came in? What went out? What was left? EU rules for company accounts call it the profit and loss account, and many people just say P&L.

The top line is <Term id="revenue">revenue</Term>: all the money that came in from sales. In Europe it is often called turnover. At your stand in August, that was 400 crêpes at €5 each, or €2,000.

**Revenue is not what you made. It is where the counting starts.**

## Gross profit: what the crêpes left behind

Each crêpe needs batter, a filling and a paper plate, €2 in all. These are <Term id="variable-cost">variable costs</Term>, because every crêpe sold needs them again. Added up for the month, they are the <Term id="cost-of-sales">cost of sales</Term>: €800 for 400 crêpes.

Revenue minus cost of sales is <Term id="gross-profit">gross profit</Term>, here €1,200. It tests the product itself. **Does each sale leave money behind before the month's other bills?** At €5 a crêpe and €2 to make, each one keeps €3, so the answer is yes.

## Net profit: after every bill

The festival charged €300 for the pitch, and the griddle and gazebo cost €150 to hire. These are <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>: the same whether you sell 40 crêpes or 400.

Take them from gross profit and you reach <Term id="net-profit">net profit</Term>, the bottom line. Here it is €750, before any tax on profit. **That is the number you and your friend can split, not the €2,000.**

## Why two kinds of profit

The two profit lines answer different questions. Gross profit asks whether the product works: does each crêpe earn more than it costs to make? Net profit asks whether the whole month worked, pitch and hire included.

So they point to different fixes. Low gross profit points at the price, or at what each crêpe costs to make. Healthy gross profit with thin net profit points lower down, at fixed costs that are too high for the sales.

Selling more helps with the second. Every extra crêpe adds €3 to gross profit. Once enough of them cover the €450, net profit starts to grow.

**Read the lines from the top, and the statement tells you where to look.**

## What the statement leaves out

A statement usually counts a sale when it is made, not when the money arrives. Say a school orders 40 crêpes for an open day and pays next month. That €200 counts in this month's revenue but is not in the account yet. That gap is <Term id="cash-flow">cash flow</Term>.

If the stand charges <Term id="vat">VAT</Term>, that part of each price is collected for the tax authority. EU accounting rules and Eurostat both leave it out of turnover.

**Tax on business profit is set by each country, not the EU.** Ask your national tax authority what applies to a small stand. If you sell in złoty, forint or krona, every line works the same.
