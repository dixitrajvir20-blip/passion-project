## Two sides of every price

Every price has two sides pushing on it. <Term id="supply">Supply</Term> is how much sellers have to sell. <Term id="demand">Demand</Term> is how much buyers want at each price.

When buyers want more than there is at today's price, that is a <Term id="shortage">shortage</Term>, and the price tends to climb. When there is more than buyers want, that is a <Term id="surplus">surplus</Term>, and the price tends to fall.

**So when a price moves, ask first: which side moved?** Then ask a second question, which this lesson keeps coming back to. When demand rises, can sellers make more, or is what they have all there is?

## When the olive harvest failed

In 2022 and 2023, drought cut several European harvests. Spain, the EU's biggest olive grower, had two poor olive harvests in a row. Eurostat put a 48% jump in farm prices for olive oil by mid-2023 down mostly to drought-hit crops.

Shoppers felt it next. In January 2024, olive oil in EU shops cost 50% more than a year earlier. It rose in every EU country, from 13% in Romania to 69% in Portugal.

**Kitchens did not suddenly want half as much oil again. The same kitchens were chasing less oil.**

## When the harvest came back

The 2024 harvest was much bigger. The EU picked 12.7 million tonnes of olives for oil, up from 9.2 million in 2023. Spain's crop grew by about two thirds.

Early in 2025, the price farmers got for olive oil was 43.5% lower than a year before. **A price pushed up by short supply usually comes down when the supply returns.** Both swings started on the supply side, in the olive groves.

## Summer: when demand moves

In summer, many more people want the same things at the same time. In 2025, 31.1% of all nights in EU tourist accommodation fell in July and August. In Croatia it was 54.5%.

Picture two sellers by the same beach. The ice-cream kiosk can get more tubs overnight, because ice cream is made ahead and kept frozen. So in a hot week it mostly sells more, at about the usual price. The guesthouse next door cannot add rooms in August.

**When supply can grow, extra demand shows up as more sold. When it cannot, it shows up in the price.**

## Tickets and drops: supply that cannot grow

A festival field holds a set number of people. When a strong line-up is announced, far more fans may want tickets than exist. Some festivals sell in tiers: when the cheapest tier sells out, the next one costs more. That is the price following demand, one step at a time.

A limited trainer drop works the other way. The brand makes a set number of pairs and fixes the price. If far more people want a pair at that price than there are pairs, it sells out in minutes. **The queue, the draw and the resale listing at twice the price all come from that shortage.**

## Resale rules differ by country

A resale price follows how many people want the thing against how many exist. It says nothing about what the thing cost to make.

What a seller may charge on resale is a national question. EU law lets each country add its own rules, such as a cap on ticket resale prices. So the answer depends on where you live, and your country's consumer authority has the details.

One rule covers the whole EU. Under a 2019 EU law, a trader may not resell event tickets bought with software that gets around the limit per buyer.
