## Who is paid when you tap

A <Term id="referral-link">referral link</Term> or code, a gift or a free account is payment. When a creator takes one, the post is <Term id="paid-promotion">paid promotion</Term>, whatever else it also is.

ESMA's factsheet for finfluencers asks for a label a follower cannot miss: the words Ad, Paid partnership or Sponsored. Small grey text will not do, and neither will a hashtag buried in a row of twenty.

**The same goes for what a creator already holds.** Someone who would gain if you bought the same thing has a <Term id="conflict-of-interest">conflict of interest</Term>, and ESMA says that belongs in the post too.

The first question to ask about a money post is who gains when you act on it.

## What the post counts as

EU market abuse rules are broad. A post that points people towards buying or selling a <Term id="financial-instrument">financial instrument</Term>, or offers a view on its future price, can be an <Term id="investment-recommendation">investment recommendation</Term>. ESMA says everyday wording changes nothing, and that educational content can count too.

Anyone posting one must give their name and the date, and mark which parts are opinion. Whatever they stand to gain goes in the post. ESMA adds that a link to it elsewhere may not be enough.

Telling one person what to do with their money goes further. That is investment advice, and it needs a licence from a national regulator.

**ESMA's factsheet says "this is not investment advice" will not protect the person posting.**

## Authorised, and where to check

Providing investment services in an EU country takes permission from a national regulator. A firm with that permission is an <Term id="authorised-firm">authorised firm</Term>. ESMA's advice to investors is to check with your own regulator whether a company is authorised.

Under EU market abuse rules each member state names one authority. ESMA publishes the list: the Central Bank of Ireland, CONSOB in Italy, the AMF in France, BaFin in Germany.

The EU's crypto rules for service providers came into full application in December 2024. ESMA's register lists the providers authorised under them, and entities found to be providing those services without permission.

**Check the firm, not the person posting about it.**

## What a risk warning is telling you

ESMA's factsheet names the investments finfluencers most often market: <Term id="contract-for-difference">contracts for difference</Term>, other <Term id="leverage">leveraged</Term> products, and volatile <Term id="crypto-asset">crypto-assets</Term>. Each can lose everything put into it.

When ESMA restricted contracts for difference in 2018, the measures included a standard <Term id="risk-warning">risk warning</Term> carrying one number: the share of that firm's own <Term id="retail-client">retail</Term> accounts that lose money.

**That number describes customers, not markets.** Studies by national regulators, reported by ESMA in 2018, found 74 to 89 of every 100 retail accounts losing money. Average losses ran from €1,600 to €29,000 per customer.

You need no account to read it, and it is the most checkable fact in the advert.

## The promised monthly figure

A promised 20% a month is the opening line of a familiar pattern. The rest of it: a private group, a small early payout that buys trust, and a deadline.

ESMA's factsheet warns that the products pushed this way can lose all the money put into them. Something that can lose everything cannot also promise a set gain every month.

**Hurry is part of the design.** Places running out, a window closing, a group that shuts tonight. The pressure is there so that nobody stops to check.

The European Parliament took up the same subject in April 2026, naming hidden advertising, conflicts of interest and misleading claims among the risks.

## When an app is really a bet

Some apps turn a price into a yes-or-no question with a fixed payout: right, a set amount comes back; wrong, nothing does. That is a <Term id="binary-option">binary option</Term>.

ESMA prohibited selling these to retail customers in 2018. Its measures could only be temporary, so regulators across the EU replaced them with national ones between 2019 and 2020. In July 2026 ESMA reminded firms that event contracts, meaning bets on whether something happens, fall under those measures when they count as financial instruments.

**Nothing is owned, and how far the price moves does not matter.** Charts, streaks and a leaderboard are borrowed from betting apps. A fixed payout or nothing is a bet, whatever the screen around it looks like.

## If money has already gone

Stop replying to them. Then report it to the police where you live, and tell your national regulator. ESMA's investor pages list those steps, along with where to search alerts about suspected scams.

Keep the firm's name, the app, the dates, the amounts and every message.

**Treat any later offer to recover the money as part of the same script.** ESMA says it never approaches people for their details to help recover lost funds. It also says any site using its name or logo, other than its own, is fake.

Tell someone at home what happened, and work through ESMA's list of steps with them.
