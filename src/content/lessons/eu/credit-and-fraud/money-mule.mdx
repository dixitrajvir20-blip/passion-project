## What the offer is paying for

The advert calls it a finance internship. The work is receiving transfers and sending them on, and the pay is a share of what passes through.

That share is not pay for work. It is the price of your bank account standing between a victim and the person who took the money. Someone who lets an account be used that way is a <Term id="money-mule">money mule</Term>.

**The money arriving has usually just been taken from someone.** Europol reports that more than 90% of the mule transactions found through its European Money Mule Actions are linked to cybercrime. Its examples include <Term id="phishing">phishing</Term>, online shopping fraud, fake emails from a company's supplier, and romance scams.

## Who gets asked

Recruiters aim at people who are looking for work. The European Banking Federation's campaign says men are targeted more often than women, and people aged 18 to 34 more often than people over 55. Students, newcomers to a country and people who are unemployed are named as more at risk.

**The adverts arrive where those people already are.** After its sixth coordinated action, in 2020, Europol described criminals turning to social media to recruit. The methods named were fake job offers, pop-up ads and messaging apps. That action identified 4,031 mules and 227 recruiters.

## The shape of the advert

The federation's campaign lists the warning signs to look for in a job advert.

| What the advert says | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Local or national representative, or agent | A title for moving money, with no trade behind it |
| The duties are never described | There is no work to describe, only transfers |
| No education or experience is asked for | Nobody is being chosen for a skill |
| Everything happens online | Nobody has to meet you, and nobody can be traced |
| High pay for little effort | The pay is for the account, not for the hours |
| The job uses your bank account to move money | This is the line the whole scheme needs |

Any one of these can turn up in a clumsy but real advert. **The last row is different: a real employer has no reason to move other people's money through your account.**

The campaign also warns about websites that copy a real company's, at a very similar web address. It warns about awkward writing sent from a free web email address.

## What a real employer does

A real employer pays you from its own name, for hours or work you did. The money stops with you. All it needs is an <Term id="iban">IBAN</Term> to pay your wages into.

Check the company before you accept. Look for duties described in writing, and a website and email address at the company's own domain. An interview and a written contract are normal to ask for.

**Wages are paid into an account, using the account number.** A card number and its <Term id="card-security-code">security code</Term> are used to take money from a card, so payroll has no reason to ask for them.

## What the law calls it

Moving money you know came from crime is <Term id="money-laundering">money laundering</Term>. The EU directive on the offence covers converting or transferring such property. It also covers acquiring, possessing or using it, knowing at the time you received it where it came from.

Member states must make that punishable by a maximum term of at least four years. They may go further, and cover a person who suspected, or ought to have known, that the money came from crime.

**So saying you did not know is not the protection it sounds like.** The federation's campaign warns that these consequences can follow even when someone acted unwittingly.

## What happens if you are caught

The European Banking Federation's campaign says a money mule who is caught can face "a prison sentence, fine or community service". It adds the prospect of never again being able to open a bank account or get a mortgage.

**In its ninth coordinated action against money muling, Europol and its partners identified 10,759 mules and 474 recruiters.** In that action 1,013 people were arrested, and 2,822 banks and financial institutions took part. The account is the step in the chain that carries a name.

## If money has already moved through your account

Stop the transfers now. Every further one adds to what has to be explained.

Then follow the steps the federation's campaign gives: tell your bank, tell the service you used, and tell the police in your country. Keep the advert, the messages, and the account details on both sides.

**Do not move the money on again to tidy it up.** The directive lists concealing or disguising the movement of criminal property as an offence in its own right.
