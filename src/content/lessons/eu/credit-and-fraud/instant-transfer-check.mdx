## Ten seconds, and no way back

An <Term id="instant-credit-transfer">instant credit transfer</Term> moves euros from your account to another within ten seconds, at any hour on any day. Since 9 October 2025, every euro-area bank that offers euro transfers must send them. It may not charge more for one than for a standard transfer.

**Once you press send, the transfer cannot be recalled.** EU payment rules treat a transfer to the <Term id="iban">IBAN</Term> you typed as correctly made, whatever name you typed beside it. Your bank must make reasonable efforts to get the money back. It cannot take it back.

That is why every check in this lesson happens before the button, not after.

## Who bears the loss

In 2024, payment fraud in the European Economic Area (the EU plus Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein) cost €4.2 billion, the ECB and the EBA report. Credit transfers made up €2.2 billion of it, 16% more than the year before.

**About 85% of the credit-transfer losses fell on the people who paid.** The main reason was not stolen cards or broken security. A scam got them to start the payment themselves.

## Two kinds of payment

An <Term id="unauthorised-payment">unauthorised payment</Term> is one you never approved: a card payment made with stolen details, say. EU rules say your bank refunds it. At most €50 of the loss can fall on you, and nothing if you could not have known, unless you were dishonest or seriously careless.

An <Term id="authorised-push-payment">authorised push payment</Term> is one you set up and approved yourself, even if a lie got you there. **The refund rule does not cover it, because the bank did what you asked.** In November 2025 the Parliament and the Council agreed new EU rules covering one kind of scam, where someone poses as your bank. They are not law yet. Your national authority knows the position today.

## The name check

Since 9 October 2025, banks in the euro area must run a <Term id="verification-of-payee">verification of payee</Term> before a transfer. Free of charge, the bank compares the name you typed with the name on the account behind the IBAN.

A match means the names agree. A close match shows the name on the account, so you can compare. A no match means that is not the name on the account. The bank must warn you, but it does not block the payment.

**A no match is the moment to stop, not to click through.** Outside the euro area, in Poland or Sweden, banks have until 9 July 2027 to offer it. Until then, the check is yours to run.

## Checks the app cannot run

The name check catches a wrong account. It cannot tell you whether the person asking is who they claim to be. Three questions do that.

Did the account details arrive by message? A changed IBAN announced by text, email or chat is checked by a route you had before the message: the number saved in your phone, the office, the old account. Never the reply button.

Are you being moved off a platform? A seller who wants a transfer instead of the platform's own payment is asking you to give up its protection before you have anything.

Is there a rush? A deadline is part of the pattern. A real payee can wait for one call.

## If money has gone

**Tell your bank as soon as you notice, before anything else.** It cannot recall the transfer, but it must try to recover the money, and the receiving bank must help.

The account you paid is often a <Term id="money-mule">money mule</Term>'s, lent or sold so that stolen money can move on. Europol's money-mule operations tie over 90% of the mule transactions they find to cybercrime. Mule accounts exist to pass money onward, so each hour lets it move further.

Then report it to the police in your country; Europol lists each country's online route. Keep the IBAN, the amount, the time and every message. Tell whoever was impersonated too, so they can warn their other contacts.
