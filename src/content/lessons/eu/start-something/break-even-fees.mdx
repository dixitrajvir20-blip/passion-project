## Where €55 goes

You repair bicycles at weekends from a rented bay in a shared workshop. A full service is booked and paid through a platform, at €55. Parts and consumables, the small supplies used up on one service, cost €22.

On paper that leaves €33. The platform's <Term id="commission">commission</Term> and the card charge take about €5 more. Each of those is a <Term id="platform-fee">platform fee</Term>.

So the booking leaves you €28. That €28 is its <Term id="contribution-margin">contribution margin</Term>: what one job adds once its own costs are paid. The fees are a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>. Take one more booking and you pay them again.

**Every charge taken from a booking belongs in the cost of that booking.**

## What the fee takes from what you keep

Five euros sounds minor next to €55. It is about 9% of the price. But it comes out of the €33 left after parts, and there it is about 15%.

That is the gap that makes fees easy to miss. You measure a fee against the price, not against what the job leaves.

The card charge is not one charge. Part of it is <Term id="interchange-fee">interchange</Term>, which goes to the bank that issued the customer's card. EU rules cap that at 0.2% on a consumer debit payment and 0.3% on a consumer credit one. The rest goes to the card network and to the firm that takes your payments.

## Fees per booking, fees per month

The bay, the insurance and the loan on your tools come to €320 a month. You pay that whether you take twelve bookings or none. Those are <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>.

Fees split the same way. A commission on each booking belongs with the parts, inside the cost of that job. A monthly charge for a seller account belongs with the bay, in fixed costs.

The sum does not change outside the euro area. A repairer in Warsaw runs the same lines in złoty.

**Put each fee on the line that matches how it is charged: per booking, or per month.**

## Twelve bookings, not ten

Without the fees, each booking would keep €33, and ten would cover the €320. With €5 of fees inside the cost, it takes twelve.

You cannot do part of a repair, so a fraction of a booking rounds up to a whole one. Twelve is your <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point for the month.

Five euros a booking moved the count by two. That is a fifth more repairs for the same fixed costs.

## After the twelfth

Once the €320 is covered, the month's fixed costs are paid. They do not grow when you take a thirteenth booking.

So the thirteenth adds its whole €28 to your profit, not €55, because parts and fees still come out of it. So does the fourteenth, and every booking after that.

The first eleven bookings all go on costs, and the twelfth finishes them. A quiet month works the same way in reverse. At eight bookings you are €96 short of the €320.

## Charging extra for paying by card

In the EU a seller cannot add a <Term id="surcharge">surcharge</Term> when a customer pays with an everyday consumer debit or credit card. Two kinds of card sit outside that rule. One is a business or company card, billed to an employer. The other is a card from a company that also takes the payment itself.

A charge that is still allowed is limited to what taking that payment costs you. Some countries ban them completely, so check the rules where you trade.

The customer has to see the full total, extras included, before the order is placed. **So the card fee belongs in the price you set, not at the checkout.**

## Check the payout, then watch for changes

After your first bookings, open the statement that shows what reached your account. Each payout should be your price minus the fees you counted. If less arrived, a charge is missing from your sum.

Fees also change. Under EU rules a platform must give its business sellers at least 15 days' notice before new terms take effect. The same rules require larger platforms to run a complaints system for their sellers.

So when notice arrives, put the new figure into the cost of one booking. Work out break-even again before the change starts.
