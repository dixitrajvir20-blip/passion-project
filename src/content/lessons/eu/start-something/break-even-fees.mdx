## Where €15 goes

You make earrings in Bologna and sell them on an online marketplace for €15 a pair. Beads, hooks, a small box and a padded envelope cost €5. The buyer pays the post on top.

On paper you keep €10. But two more hands reach into every sale. In this example the marketplace charges €1.50 for each sale. The payment service that takes the buyer's card charges €0.50 an order. Each of these is a <Term id="platform-fee">platform fee</Term>.

So each pair leaves you €8, not €10. That €8 is your real <Term id="margin">margin</Term>. The fees are a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: sell one more pair, and you pay them one more time.

**Put every fee charged per sale into the cost of one sale.**

## Small beside the price, big beside what you keep

Two euros sounds small next to a €15 price. It is about 13% of it. But the fees come out of the €10 you would have kept, and there they are a fifth.

Your monthly costs are €120: €100 for a bench at a shared makers' space, and €20 for the shop's monthly plan. These are <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>. The plan is a platform fee too, but it is charged monthly. **Fees per sale go into the cost of one sale. Fees per month go into fixed costs.**

Without the fees, 12 pairs would cover the €120. With them, it takes 15. A small fee can move your <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point by a quarter.

## Percentage fees grow with the price

Some marketplaces take a percentage of each sale, instead of a flat amount or as well as one. Say a platform takes 10%. On a €15 pair that is €1.50. Raise the price to €20 and the fee becomes €2.

So a price rise of €5 brings you €4.50, not €5. **With a percentage fee, part of every price rise goes to the platform.**

Check the fee page for what the percentage is taken from. It may be the item price alone, or the item plus the post the buyer pays. The same 10% costs you more when it is taken from both.

## Flat fees weigh most on small orders

A flat fee per order works the other way. It stays the same whatever the order is worth. Fifty cents is a thirtieth of a €15 order, but a sixth of a €3 sticker.

So cheap items sold one at a time lose the most to flat fees. A pack of five stickers pays the fee once instead of five times. That is one reason sellers put small things in packs, or set a minimum order.

If you sell in złoty or koruna, every step is the same.

## Why the fee goes inside the price

You might want to add the card fee to the buyer's total. That extra is a <Term id="surcharge">surcharge</Term>. In the EU, a seller cannot add one when a buyer pays with an everyday debit or credit card, at home or in any other EU country. A few card types, such as company cards, fall outside this rule.

Where a charge for paying a certain way is allowed, it can only cover what that payment really costs you. Some countries allow none at all, so check your own country's rules first.

Whatever you charge, the buyer must see the full total, extras included, before placing the order. **The simplest plan is a price that already carries your fees.**

## Check what reaches you

After your first few sales, open the statement that shows what reached your account. Each payout should be your price minus the fees you counted. If less arrived, a fee is missing from your sum.

Platforms also change their fees from time to time. When a notice arrives, put the new fee into the cost of one sale. Then work out break-even again, before the new fee starts.
