## When selling becomes a business

Selling a few drawings to friends feels like a hobby. A venue that wants a poster every week, and an <Term id="invoice">invoice</Term> each month, feels different. Somewhere between the two, the law may see a business.

**Each EU country sets its own rules on when you must register, so there is no single EU answer.** Two people selling the same amount in Spain and in Poland can be told different things.

The easy slip is borrowing an answer. A friend's rule from another country is a fact about that country, not yours.

## Three questions, in order

You do not need to know the rules before you start. You need the right questions, asked in the right order.

| Order | Question to ask | What the answer decides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Do I need to register, and where? | Whether you are running a business in law, and which offices need to know |
| 2 | Does VAT apply to me? | Whether your prices and invoices include VAT |
| 3 | What records must I keep, and for how long? | What you write down for every sale and cost |

**Registering comes first, because the other answers depend on it.** One common form is the <Term id="sole-trader">sole trader</Term>: a business in your own name, with no company behind it. Ask which forms your country offers.

Then <Term id="bookkeeping">bookkeeping</Term>. Keep a dated record of every sale and every cost, however it was paid. Cash at a market counts. Ask how long the records must be kept.

## VAT is its own question

<Term id="vat">VAT</Term> is a tax on sales, and registering a business does not settle it. Each country sets a yearly sales limit, no higher than €85,000, under which a small business can choose not to charge VAT. Your country's limit may be lower.

A business using this exemption charges no VAT and shows none on its invoices. It also cannot claim back the VAT it pays on paper, ink or tools.

**So the first VAT question is not which rate to charge. It is whether you charge VAT at all.** Your national tax authority has the answer.

## Where to ask

Start with Your Europe, the European Commission's guide for people and businesses. Its business pages explain the shared EU rules and point on to each country.

Every EU country also runs a <Term id="point-of-single-contact">point of single contact</Term>. It is an official site that lists what a new business needs there, such as registration and permits. Many let you complete the steps online. For VAT and income tax, go to your national tax authority.

**Write down each answer, the date and who gave it.** Rules change, and a dated note shows what you were told. If you sell in złoty, koruna or forint, the questions are the same.

## If you are under 18

Across the EU, you become an adult in law at 18. Before that, your country decides what you can sign or run on your own. In Germany, for example, a minor can be allowed to run a business with the agreement of their parents and a court.

**So add a fourth question: can I register at my age, and what must a parent sign?** Ask it with a parent or guardian beside you, since the answer may need their agreement.

A false date of birth on a form or in an app does not answer the question. It only hides it.
