## Four sites, and one top line

You invoiced four sites at €600 in March. That €2,400 is <Term id="revenue">revenue</Term>, the top line of an <Term id="income-statement">income statement</Term>: what the work sold for, before a single cost is taken off.

An income statement covers one stretch of time, here one month. EU accounting rules call it the profit and loss account, often shortened to P&L.

**Revenue is where the counting starts, not what you made.** The rest of the statement is the costs, in two groups, and what each group leaves behind.

## What the four jobs used up

Stock images, the hosting bought for those sites and two licensed fonts cost €300. That is <Term id="cost-of-sales">cost of sales</Term>: what the jobs themselves used up. None of it would have been bought without the four jobs.

Revenue minus cost of sales is <Term id="gross-profit">gross profit</Term>, here €2,100. **It tests the work itself: does a site leave money behind before the month's own bills?**

Spread over the four sites, that €300 is €75 each. So a €600 site leaves €525, and four of them leave €2,100.

## The costs of the month itself

Then come the costs of running the month: software at €90, a shared desk at €400, and €160 of train fares to clients. Together that is €650 of <Term id="operating-expenses">operating expenses</Term>.

Two of the three are <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>. The subscription and the desk are charged whether four sites arrive or none, so €490 is owed even in a month with no work.

Take the €650 from gross profit and you reach <Term id="net-profit">net profit</Term> before tax: €1,450. **That is what the month left you, and it is the bottom line.**

## What the bottom line is for

Tax on business profit starts from profit, not from the money clients paid in. Each EU country sets its own rules and rates, so what you owe and what you have to file depends on where you are. Ask your national tax authority.

Who has to produce a statement depends on how the business is set up. A <Term id="limited-liability-company">limited liability company</Term> keeps yearly accounts in every EU country. The profit and loss account is one of the three parts of those accounts, beside the balance sheet and the notes. If you are <Term id="self-employed">self-employed</Term> with no company, the rules that apply depend on your country. **The three lines are still how you know whether the month paid.**

## Earned in March, paid in April

The March statement counts a sale when the work is <Term id="invoice">invoiced</Term>, not when the money lands. Eurostat measures a period's sales the same way: the totals billed in it. A client who pays on 15 April still belongs in March. **That gap between earning and being paid is what <Term id="cash-flow">cash flow</Term> tracks, and it is why a profitable month can leave a thin account.**

If you charge <Term id="vat">VAT</Term>, that part of the price is not yours to count. The buyer pays it inside the price and you hand it on, so a top line leaves it out. Each EU country sets its own rates, and a standard rate cannot be below 15%.

## Why one month is not enough

March had four sites in it. August may have one, and the desk and the subscription are charged in both.

So read three or four months together before trusting a figure. Write each cost as a share of revenue, the way the calculation below does. A quiet month then shows itself at once: the same €490 is about a fifth of a €2,400 month and half of a €980 one.

Business money and personal money are also easier to read apart than together. The EU and OECD framework for 16 to 18 year olds lists keeping them separate as a skill of its own.
