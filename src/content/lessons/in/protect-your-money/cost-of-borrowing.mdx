## Turn the fee into a rate

An app offers ₹5,000 for seven days and takes ₹500 for it. A single fee sounds flat, so it reads as small.

It is not flat. ₹500 on ₹5,000 is 10% of what you borrowed, and it buys one week. A year holds about 52 of those weeks.

**A fee becomes a rate once you divide it by the amount and stretch it over a year.** At 10% a week, the simple yearly rate is about 521%.

Two steps, on any offer. Divide the charge by the sum borrowed. Then multiply by how many of those periods fit in a year. <Term id="interest">Interest</Term> quoted by the month needs only the second step. Every rate below is an example.

| Offer | The rate it names | The same cost, simple, over a year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ₹500 to borrow ₹5,000 for 7 days | 10% for the week | about 521% |
| A card at 3.5% a month | 3.5% a month | 42% |
| A loan at 3% a month | 3% a month | 36% |

## What the lender must show you first

Before you sign a digital loan, an RBI-regulated lender gives you a <Term id="key-facts-statement">Key Facts Statement</Term>. It lists the amount, each repayment, every charge, and the <Term id="apr">APR</Term>.

APR is the yearly cost of the credit, with the interest and every charge folded in. **It is the one number on which two offers can be compared.** A charge left off the statement cannot be added later without your clear agreement.

Then comes a <Term id="cooling-off-period">cooling-off period</Term>, at least one day long. Inside it you can hand the loan back. You repay what you borrowed and the cost for those days, and no penalty is charged. A processing fee the statement listed may be kept.

## Checking who is behind an app

The app is rarely the lender. The money comes from a bank or an <Term id="nbfc">NBFC</Term>, a finance company registered with the Reserve Bank of India.

**The Key Facts Statement comes on the lender's own letterhead, so the lender is named before you sign.** Where an app lists several lenders' offers, each must name the lender behind it. RBI also publishes a directory of lending apps, reported by the lenders themselves. Being listed is not RBI approval.

RBI has warned that unauthorised apps charge high interest, add charges the borrower never saw, and collect harshly. Its rules bar a lawful app from reading your contacts, files or call logs. Complaints about an unregulated lender go to RBI's Sachet portal, sachet.rbi.org.in.

## The minimum due is not the bill

A card statement names two figures: what you owe, and a <Term id="minimum-amount-due">minimum amount due</Term>. Paying the minimum by the due date counts as on time. The rest stays owed, and it carries interest.

On the ₹20,000 above, at an example rate of 3.5% a month, the month's interest is ₹700. The ₹1,000 minimum covers that first. **Only ₹300 of it comes off the ₹20,000.**

RBI requires the minimum to be at least the month's interest and charges, so the balance cannot grow while you pay it. RBI's wording for every statement says that paying only the minimum leaves "repayment stretching over months / years with consequential compounded interest payment".

## What reaches your credit report

Lenders and card issuers send your repayment record to the <Term id="credit-information-company">credit information companies</Term> every fortnight. A late payment or a default is reported, and you are sent an alert when it is.

That record becomes your <Term id="credit-report">credit report</Term>. **An entry goes in whether you paid on time or not, and the next lender reads it.**

You can ask each company for one free full credit report, with the score, once in a calendar year. Read it. If something in it is wrong, a correction goes through the company or the lender, and the rules give them 30 days.
