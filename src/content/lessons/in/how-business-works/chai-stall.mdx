## One cup at a time

Forget the queue for a moment and look at a single cup. The customer pays ₹15. Tea leaves, milk, sugar, ginger, the gas to boil it and the paper cup cost about ₹8. The stall keeps ₹7.

That ₹7 is the <Term id="margin">margin</Term>. Every decision about a small business starts here. **If one sale does not leave money behind, a thousand sales will not either.**

The ₹8 is a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: make more cups, spend more on milk and gas.

## The costs that do not care

Some costs arrive whatever happens. The fee for the spot on the pavement, the vending licence, the monthly instalment on the loan that bought the cart. Call it ₹2,000 a month. The food safety registration costs nothing to ₹100 a year, so leave it out. These are <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>, and they are owed on a rainy Tuesday with four customers just as much as on a busy Friday.

**So the ₹7 from each cup has a job before it is profit.** It pays down the ₹2,000 first. The cup that finishes that job is the <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point.

## Why the busy stall is short of cash

Now the twist the sums leave out. Many regulars do not pay per cup. They drink all week and settle on Saturday. That is <Term id="udhaar">udhaar</Term>, the same credit a <Hi>kirana</Hi> shop gives its regulars.

But the milk supplier wants cash on Wednesday. **A stall can be in profit for the month and still have an empty cash box midweek.** That gap between earning money and holding it is <Term id="cash-flow">cash flow</Term>, and it can close a business that is doing well.

So the owner keeps cash ready for tomorrow's milk. That is the stall's <Term id="working-capital">working capital</Term>. A <Term id="upi">UPI</Term> QR code helps a little: a regular with no change can still pay today, and the record shows who has paid.
