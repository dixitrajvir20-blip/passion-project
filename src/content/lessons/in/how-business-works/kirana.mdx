## One bag of atta

Start with atta, which most of your aunt's customers buy. A 5 kg bag has ₹270 printed on it. The shop paid the distributor ₹255, so it keeps ₹15. That ₹15 is the <Term id="margin">margin</Term>.

Why so little? Almost every shop nearby sells atta, and customers buy it weekly, so they know the price. With many sellers, the price gets pushed close to its cost. The shop also cannot charge more than the <Term id="mrp">MRP</Term>, the price printed on the pack. **Atta brings her regulars in, but each bag leaves very little behind.**

Then come the <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>: rent, electricity for the fridge, the phone. Call it ₹15,000 a month, before your aunt is paid anything for her long day.

## Where the better margins are

Now look at the namkeen. Your aunt buys it in big bags and weighs out 250 g at the counter for ₹50. Those 250 g cost her ₹30, so she keeps ₹20. There is no printed price to check it against, and the shop next door may not sell the same mix.

A ₹50 packet keeps more than a ₹270 bag. It helps to see it as a share of the price, the <Term id="gross-margin">gross margin</Term>. On the atta, the shop keeps less than 6 paise of every rupee. On the namkeen, it keeps 40 paise.

**Many of her regulars come in for atta and leave with namkeen, soap or a cold drink too.** That mix is what pays the rent.

## Money sitting on the shelf

Your aunt paid cash for every packet on her shelves before anyone bought it. Until it sells, that money is stuck. This is the shop's <Term id="stock">stock</Term>.

Some stock also has a date. Say a loaf of bread costs the shop ₹40 and sells for ₹45. Past the use-by date on its label, it cannot be sold. **If the supplier will not take it back, one wasted loaf loses the ₹40 the shop paid: the ₹5 kept on each of eight loaves that did sell.**

So a careful shopkeeper orders bread and milk in small amounts, often. Older packets go in front, so they sell first. A bulk deal on something slow to sell can end up in the bin.

## The udhaar book and the cash box

Many of her regulars buy on <Term id="udhaar">udhaar</Term>. They take groceries all month and settle when their salary arrives. Your aunt writes it in a notebook. Today that notebook holds ₹20,000: money earned, but not yet paid.

The distributor's van comes every week and wants cash for new stock. So the shop's money sits in two places at once: on the shelves and in the notebook. **Your aunt can make a profit this month and still have no cash on the day the van comes.** That is a <Term id="cash-flow">cash flow</Term> problem.

The cash a <Hi>kirana</Hi> needs to keep its shelves full while it waits to be paid is its <Term id="working-capital">working capital</Term>. More udhaar raises it. Credit from the distributor lowers it.
