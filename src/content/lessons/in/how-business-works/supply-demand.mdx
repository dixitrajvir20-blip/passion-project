## Two sides of every price

At a vegetable market, nobody sets the price alone. Two things push on it. <Term id="supply">Supply</Term> is how much is on offer: the crates that reached the market today. <Term id="demand">Demand</Term> is how much buyers want at each price.

When supply falls and buyers want the same amount, they compete for fewer kilos and the price climbs. When too much arrives, sellers compete for the same buyers and the price drops. **A price moves when one side moves, so the first question is always: which side?**

Most vegetables start at a <Term id="mandi">mandi</Term>, the wholesale market where farmers and traders sell to shop and cart owners before dawn.

## When rain cuts the supply

In September and October 2019, late and long rains damaged India's monsoon-season onion crop. The government's own monthly figures show what came next. The average retail price across India was about ₹21 a kilo in July. By November it was about ₹61, nearly three times as much.

Nobody started eating three times as many onions. **The same buyers were chasing far fewer onions.** The reverse happens at harvest time. When a whole crop comes in at once, the glut can pull the price down hard.

## The tomato summer of 2023

Tomatoes follow a calendar. The biggest harvest comes from December to February. July, August, October and November are lean months, when less is picked. In July 2023 the monsoon made a lean month worse: moving crates got harder, and more tomatoes spoilt on the way.

The jump was big enough to lift India's <Term id="inflation">inflation</Term> figure for July, the Reserve Bank's bulletin noted. A government notice that July expected new crop in August, and prices did fall. The government's discounted tomato sale started at ₹90 a kilo on 14 July. It was cut in steps to ₹40 by 20 August, as market prices declined.

**A price pushed up by a short supply usually comes down when the next crop arrives.**

## When demand moves instead

Sometimes the crop is fine and the buyers change. Picture the week before Diwali. Flower growers pick about the usual amount, but far more homes want marigolds on the same few days. The price rises, then falls back once the festival passes.

A swap works the same way. The Reserve Bank's bulletin noted that the July 2023 tomato jump spread to other vegetables. When one vegetable gets very dear, many kitchens use a <Term id="substitute">substitute</Term>, and that extra demand can lift its price too.

**A festival or a swap lifts demand for days or weeks. A damaged crop cuts supply until the next harvest.**

## Closing time at the stall

At 9 pm, a vegetable seller cuts the price of spinach and coriander, but not onions. Nothing about the spinach changed since noon except the time left to sell it.

Leafy greens are <Term id="perishable">perishable</Term>. By morning they wilt, and few people will pay the full price for them. The seller paid for them at the mandi at dawn, so that money is spent whatever happens next. **At closing time the choice is a lower price tonight or nothing tomorrow, and a lower price is more than nothing.**

Onions and potatoes keep for weeks. There is no rush to sell them tonight, so their price holds.
