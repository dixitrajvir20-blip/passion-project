## What one order really costs

Look at one order. A pair of earrings sells for ₹250. Beads, hooks, a box and an envelope cost ₹90. That leaves ₹160.

Then you post it. Say the courier charges ₹70 for a small parcel. Now one pair leaves ₹90, and that ₹90 is your <Term id="margin">margin</Term>.

**Delivery is part of what one sale costs, even when the buyer never sees it.** Like the beads, it is a <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term>: every extra parcel adds another ₹70. India's Class 12 business studies textbook counts getting a product to the buyer as part of its cost, next to making it.

Your phone data and tools cost the same each month, however many pairs you sell. Those are <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term>.

## Free delivery is never free

You can put delivery inside the price, or show it as its own charge. Either works, as long as it is counted somewhere.

**Say the full price, delivery included, before anyone pays.** A charge that turns up after payment feels like a trick, even when it is fair.

Parcels to far-away places often cost more to post. Say that in the post too: one price nearby, and a stated extra for further away. When the courier changes its rates, redo the sum.

## A post a stranger can trust

A buyer cannot pick up your earrings, so the post has to do what your hands would. Use photos you took yourself, in daylight, with a coin or a hand for size. Put the price and the delivery charge in the same post. Say how many days posting takes, and what happens if a pair arrives broken.

**Every question a buyer has to ask is a place to give up.** Answer them before they are asked.

Share messages from happy buyers only with their OK, with names and numbers hidden. Reply when you said you would. On a small page, being reliable is most of the brand.

## Paid means in your own account

To be paid by <Term id="upi">UPI</Term>, you need only your UPI ID or your own <Term id="qr-code">QR code</Term>. The buyer types or scans it and pays. Your only job is to check.

**A sale is paid when the money shows in your own UPI or bank app, and not before.** A screenshot is a picture, and pictures can be edited. A text saying you have been credited can come from anyone.

UPI money usually lands within seconds. So open your app and find the credit before you pack. A real buyer will not mind the minute it takes.

## The fake buyer's few tricks

A fake buyer mostly tries to make you pay them. Your <Term id="upi-pin">UPI PIN</Term> only ever sends money, so any step that needs it is money leaving you.

The tricks repeat. A code sent to you so that you can receive: scanning only ever pays. A <Term id="collect-request">collect request</Term> with a note saying approve to receive. A screenshot with a rush. An <Term id="overpayment-scam">overpayment</Term>, where the buyer claims to have sent too much and wants the extra back.

India's national cybercrime centre warns that criminals send or swap codes to steer payments to themselves. **Whatever the story, check your own app before anything leaves you.** If money has gone, call 1930 or report at cybercrime.gov.in straight away.

## Cash on delivery moves the risk to you

With <Term id="cash-on-delivery">cash on delivery</Term>, the buyer pays the courier at the door. The courier passes the money on to you later. Some buyers trust it more on a first order from a stranger.

The risk sits with you. If the buyer refuses the parcel, you usually pay to send it and again to get it back. At ₹70 each way that is ₹140, and you receive nothing. **One refused parcel costs more than the ₹90 a good sale leaves.**

Money arriving days later also squeezes your <Term id="cash-flow">cash flow</Term>: next week's beads need paying for now. Two fixes: keep cash on delivery for buyers who have ordered before, or ask a new buyer to pay the delivery charge first.
