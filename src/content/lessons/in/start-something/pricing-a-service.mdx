## Forty hours, not twenty-two

Building the five pages takes about 22 hours. Revisions take 9 more. Calls, and collecting the clinic's text and photographs, take 5. Setting up the domain, the hosting and the handover takes 4. That is 40 hours for a job that looks like 22.

At ₹8,000, once ₹2,600 of costs come off, those 40 hours pay ₹135 an hour. **The 18 hours the client never sees are paid out of the same fee.**

So count the hours first. Then build the price on three things: what the job buys, the month it belongs to, and the tax that follows.

## What the job buys, and what the month costs

The domain is ₹900 for the year, hosting ₹1,200, and stock images ₹500. That is ₹2,600 bought for this site alone: the <Term id="variable-cost">variable cost</Term> of the job.

Other costs arrive whether or not a client calls. A laptop is being paid off at ₹9,900 a month. Internet and phone come to ₹1,700. Design and code subscriptions are ₹3,000, and a desk at a shared office is ₹4,000. Those <Term id="fixed-cost">fixed costs</Term> are ₹18,600 a month.

At ₹15,000 the job keeps ₹12,400. That is your <Term id="margin">margin</Term>, and it pays down the ₹18,600 first. Two sites at that price cover the month, which is the <Term id="break-even">break-even</Term> point.

## The client may pay less than the invoice

Some clients deduct tax from a professional fee before they pay it. That is <Term id="tds">TDS</Term>, tax deducted at source. For each kind of payment, the law names which payers must deduct, above what amount, and at what rate.

Say the clinic deducts ₹1,500 as an example. It deposits that against your <Term id="pan">PAN</Term>, so ₹13,500 reaches your account.

**The ₹1,500 is neither a charge nor a loss.** It is income tax paid in advance in your name.

Since 1 April 2026 these rules sit in sections 392 and 393 of the Income-tax Act, 2025. The department says the rates and limits carried over unchanged from the old Act.

## Checking the deduction, and the tax that follows

Check it rather than take it on trust. The <Term id="annual-information-statement">Annual Information Statement</Term> on the income tax portal lists the tax deducted in your name. It takes your feedback where an entry looks wrong. <Term id="form-26as">Form 26AS</Term>, on the department's TRACES portal, carries the same data. You claim that credit in the return for the year the income belongs to.

Your receipts from this job are the ₹15,000 on the <Term id="invoice">invoice</Term>, not the ₹13,500 that arrived. Working for yourself makes you <Term id="self-employed">self-employed</Term> for tax. Tax follows the income from the work, not the fee. For some listed professions, <Term id="presumptive-taxation">presumptive taxation</Term> counts half of the year's receipts as the income. The department's ITR-4 page sets out who qualifies and the limits.

## One price, with a written scope

A <Term id="flat-fee">flat fee</Term> is one price for a whole job, agreed before the work starts. It suits work with a clear end, such as a five-page site.

Finish in 30 hours rather than 40 and the fee is the same, so each hour earns more. An <Term id="hourly-rate">hourly rate</Term> would have cut the bill instead.

Write down what the fee covers: five pages, one round of changes, and the handover. That short <Term id="scope-of-work">scope of work</Term> is what lets you price extra work rather than absorb it.

## Before you send the quote

Four numbers, in that order. Every hour the job takes, including the ones the client will not see. What the job buys. A share of the month's standing costs. Then the tax, both the part a client may deduct before payment and the part due on the income.

Then set the figure per hour against what an hour of your time is worth elsewhere. At ₹15,000, the 40 hours pay ₹310 each before the month's costs. At ₹8,000 they pay ₹135.
