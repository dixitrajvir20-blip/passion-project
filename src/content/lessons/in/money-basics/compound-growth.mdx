## Growth on growth

Say you put ₹1,000 somewhere that pays <Term id="interest">interest</Term> at an example rate of 7% a year. After one year it has earned ₹70, so there is ₹1,070.

In year two, the 7% is worked out on ₹1,070, not on ₹1,000. That year earns ₹74.90. The extra ₹4.90 is interest earned on last year's interest.

That is <Term id="compound-growth">compound growth</Term>. The Reserve Bank of India's awareness booklet calls it interest on interest. **Each year the amount that earns is bigger, so each year adds a little more than the last.**

The ₹1,000 you started with is the <Term id="principal">principal</Term>. Everything above it is growth. Here it is counted once a year to keep the sums easy. The calculator below counts every month.

## Time does most of the work

Back to the question at the top. Meera saves ₹500 a month from 18 to 28, then stops adding. Kabir saves ₹500 a month from 28 to 38. Each puts in ₹60,000, at the same example rate of 7%.

At 28, Meera has about ₹86,500. She adds nothing more, but it keeps growing for ten years. **At 38 she has about ₹1,74,000, and Kabir has about ₹86,500.** The same money went in. Hers simply had ten more years to grow.

That is what waiting for a bigger salary costs. The first rupees you put aside are the ones with the longest time to grow.

## The rate is an example

Every rate in this lesson is an example, not a promise. Rates on offer change over time. A higher rate usually comes with more risk, including the chance of getting back less than you put in.

Now compare what the rate changes with what time changes. ₹500 a month for 10 years at 7% comes to about ₹86,500. At 9%, it comes to about ₹96,800. Keep 7% but save for 20 years, and it comes to about ₹2,60,000.

**Going from 7% to 9% added about ₹10,000. Ten extra years added over ₹1,70,000, and ₹60,000 of that was your own deposits.** You control when you start and how much you put in. The rate is mostly out of your hands.

## Prices grow too

Your balance grows in rupees. But prices also rise over time, and that is <Term id="inflation">inflation</Term>. So what counts is what the money can buy later, not only the number on the screen.

A rough check is to take the rise in prices away from the growth rate. Money growing at 7% while prices rise 5% buys about 2% more each year. Cash kept at home grows by nothing, so it buys a little less every year that prices rise.

**Where you keep your savings is your choice, and this lesson does not pick a place for you.**
